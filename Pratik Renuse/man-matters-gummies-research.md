# Man Matters gummies: manufacturers, founders, testing, credibility

Research compiled 11 August 2026. Every load-bearing claim is sourced. Where something could not be verified, it says so explicitly rather than being smoothed over.

---

## The short version

Man Matters is a marketing company with a supplement attached, not a supplement company with marketing attached. That is not an insult, it is what the audited numbers say: advertising has been the single largest cost line every year on record, running 36 to 67 percent of revenue.

The gummies are made by third-party contract manufacturers in Telangana. Mosaic Wellness owns no factories.

On credibility, the brand runs two different evidence regimes under one roof. Its Shilajit line publishes per-batch NABL lab reports and cites clinical studies. Its Biotin Hair Gummies, the flagship, publish no lab report, no clinical study, and do not disclose how much biotin is in the product.

The biotin dose, recovered from the 1mg label transcription and confirmed by a Tira product title, is **30 mcg per gummy**. A typical Indian competitor hair gummy runs 5,000 to 10,000 mcg. One boiled egg has roughly 10 mcg.

### What this means

Which manufacturer makes which SKU today is genuinely unresolved. Man Matters' own site says Advama; 1mg and PharmEasy say Spectrum. Marketplace listings lag label changes, so both may have been true at different times. Any specific claim needs to cite the exact listing and date.

None of the large, well-known Indian nutraceutical CDMOs (Zeon, Akums, Aeon, Sami-Sabinsa, Vasu) appear anywhere on any Man Matters label.

FSSAI licence numbers could not be validated against FoSCoS, which has no queryable public API.

## 2. Who runs it

**Mosaic Wellness Private Limited**, CIN U52609MH2019PTC328779, incorporated 1 August 2019 (formerly Astrea Body Sciences Pvt Ltd), registered at Thane One, DIL Complex, Majiwada, Thane West 400610. Man Matters launched commercially May 2020. Man Matters, Be Bodywise, Little Joys and Root Labs are brands, not separate legal entities. No subsidiaries. Auditor S.R. Batliboi and Associates LLP.

Oracle Financial Services, then investment banking, then Rebel Foods (Faasos) as a CXO, widely described as CMO though the title is not confirmed in a primary source. Partner at Kstart, Kalaari Capital's seed fund, 2018 to 2019, leading B2C investments.

**Dhyanesh Shah, co-founder.** BE Mumbai, MBA IIM Ahmedabad 2006 to 2008 as an industry scholar. Oracle Financial Services, then Engagement Manager at McKinsey, then roughly six years as Principal at Eight Roads Ventures (Fidelity's VC arm).

Four things fall out of this table.

1. **Advertising is the largest cost line in every year on record**, 35 to 42 percent of total expenditure. It has never been displaced by cost of goods, people, or logistics. A mature FMCG player runs 8 to 15 percent. Even Indian D2C peers at scale typically sit at 20 to 30.

2. **Gross margin is roughly 70 to 73 percent.** Revenue Rs 736 Cr against goods cost of roughly Rs 195 to 220 Cr. The business earns premium FMCG margins and spends essentially all of that margin acquiring customers.

3. **Total customer-acquisition load in FY25** was advertising (267.5) plus commission (84.1) plus freight (91.6), or Rs 443 Cr, **60 percent of operating revenue.**

4. **R&D does not appear as a named cost line anywhere.** Products are described as "formulated by in-house doctors." Employee benefit expense grew 21 percent in FY25 while advertising grew 93 percent.

Revenue is almost entirely product sales: Rs 734.7 Cr of Rs 736.1 Cr. Doctor consultations, 150-plus doctors and a claimed 100,000 consults a month, are an acquisition and prescription-generation funnel, not a revenue line.

The company told Entrackr in March 2026 that it "has been profitable for more than a year." That is unaudited and sits awkwardly against the audited FY25 loss. It most likely means monthly or EBITDA profitability. FY26 audited numbers should surface around October or November 2026.

To be fair: the direction of travel is real. Ad spend as a share of revenue has fallen every year, and near-breakeven at Rs 736 Cr is a genuine achievement. The point is not that the company is failing. The point is what it optimises for.

## 3. What is actually in the gummies

### The headline number

**Biotin Hair Gummies contain 30 mcg of biotin per gummy.**

Source: the 1mg label transcription, which declares the full panel, corroborated by Tira, whose product title for the Be Bodywise equivalent is literally "Be Bodywise Biotin Hair Gummies **30mcg**."

Full declared panel per gummy: biotin 30 mcg, vitamin C 20 mg, vitamin E 5 mg, vitamin A 396 mcg (as retinyl palmitate), zinc citrate 2 mg, B6 1.54 mg, B12 1 mcg, B9 60 mcg, vitamin D2 7.5 mcg, K2-7 27.5 mcg, Sesbania grandiflora extract 10,000 mcg, DHT blocker blend 10 mg.

**30 mcg is the NIH Adequate Intake for adults.** It is not a therapeutic hair dose. It is a dietary baseline that most people already hit from food.

**Man Matters' own product page never states the biotin quantity.** I fetched the 30N page directly on 11 August 2026 to confirm this. The ingredient list gives a milligram figure for exactly one thing, the DHT blocker, and lists "Vitamin B7 (D-Biotin)" bare. A product named "Biotin Hair Gummies" does not disclose how much biotin it contains, anywhere on the brand's own page.

There is a contradictory legacy Flipkart listing titled "Man Matters 5000 Mcg Biotin Gummies," now unavailable, which declares 5,000 mcg in one field and 300,000 mcg in another. Three mutually incompatible numbers, one of them physically absurd. Read that as marketplace data incoherence, not a current claim.

### Three formulas, one product name, and the expensive pack is the weak one

| SKU | Price | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| 30N | Rs 549 | Pectin, FOS base, DHT blocker 10mg, Sesbania, full B/D/K stack, black carrot colour, no sugar |
| 60N Regular | Rs 749 (out of stock) | **Sugar and liquid glucose first two ingredients**, no DHT blocker |
| 90N no added sugar | Rs 1,049 | **No DHT blocker, no Sesbania, no B6/B9/B12/D2/K2** |
| 120N | Rs 1,449 | Same thinner formula as 90N |

The Rs 1,449 pack is materially weaker than the Rs 549 pack, sold under the same name on the same page template with the same 4.0 rating displayed.

Gelling agent is pectin throughout, no gelatin anywhere in the range. Sweetening in the no-added-sugar SKUs is fructooligosaccharides, a prebiotic fibre, not maltitol or sucralose. Mannitol appears in the magnesium gummy. Maltitol and sorbitol appear in the Be Bodywise ACV gummy, unverified on a first-party page.

### The claims, verbatim

From the 30N page, unsourced and unfootnoted:

> "Use it consistently for 90 days to see visible reduction in hairfall... **93% men saw reduced hairfall in 90 days.** Complete your hair nutrition. Now."

No study, sample size, design or citation is attached anywhere. No substantiation for the 93 percent figure exists on the open web.

The FAQ on the same page walks it back:

> "**While these gummies might not directly help with hair growth**, they do help nourish your scalp and strength your hair follicles."

And attributes the DHT mechanism to zinc, not to the ingredient literally named DHT Blocker:

> "Adequate Zinc consumption helps keep DHT in check, helping with this."

Zinc content is 2 mg against an RDA of roughly 11 mg.

The claimed timeline also differs between SKUs for the same product. The 120N page promises results a full month earlier than the 60N page at every stage.

### The trust badges

On the 90N and 120N: "No Sugar Added," "100% Vegetarian," "Cruelty-Free," "Vegan," "Gluten Free," "Gelatin Free," "Artificial Colour Free," "Non-GMO," and **"Australian Made Safe Certified."** That last one, an Australian certification on a Telangana-manufactured product, was not verified against the MADE SAFE registry.

On the multivitamin: "Clinically Tested" as a badge with no study cited, and "Certified by Nutritionists" with no nutritionist named.

The brand states FSSAI licence numbers but never uses the phrase "FSSAI approved." That is correct and to their credit, since FSSAI licenses rather than approves.

### Where they do it right: Shilajit

The Shilajit line is a different animal. It publishes:

- Four certificate types: fulvic acid, aflatoxin, heavy metal, clinical
- Per-batch lab reports retrievable by batch number at manmatters.com/habit/honest-report
- "Certified for Purity, Safety, and Quality by NABL Accredited Labs"
- Full quantitative ingredient disclosure, every extract in milligrams

This is meaningfully above Indian category norm. Which makes the total absence of a biotin figure on the biotin product more conspicuous, not less.

Their Shilajit claims are also more falsifiable, and worth checking: "25% improvement in Fitness Score, 23% reduction in cortisol" across two 90-day studies, and "we have contributed 2 of the only 8 clinical studies on Shilajit that exist globally." Neither paper was locatable in PubMed. They may sit in non-indexed journals, which is itself a signal, since FSSAI requires peer-reviewed publication but not indexing.

## 4. Testing and credibility

### What exists

| ClinicalTrials.gov, sponsor Mosaic Wellness | **Empty response** |
| CTRI (Clinical Trials Registry India) | **Unverified.** POST-only search rejected automated queries. Do not claim "none exists" |

| COA for Shilajit gummies | **Published**, per batch, NABL labs |
| Substantiation for the 93% figure | **None found anywhere** |

### Why this is legal

India's product-approval regime for nutraceuticals was quashed by the Supreme Court in August 2015. Since then there is **no pre-market approval**. The model is licence plus self-declared compliance, verified only by post-market sampling.

Under the FSS (Advertising and Claims) Regulations 2018, health claims must be scientifically substantiated by statistically significant clinical results published in a peer-reviewed journal. But nutrition-content and general health claims meeting Schedule I need **no prior approval**. Only reduction-of-disease-risk claims outside the defined criteria need clearance.

In practice: a brand asserts its claim is substantiated, ships the product, and the substantiation is only tested if someone complains or FSSAI samples it.

**Maximum penalty for misbranding under Section 52 is Rs 3 lakh.** Against a Rs 267 crore annual ad budget.

That is the real story here, and it is bigger than one brand: the Indian regulatory architecture makes it economically rational to market efficacy you have never tested.

Two specific flags on this label under that framework. An ingredient literally named "DHT Blocker" on a food product is a physiological assertion, exactly the territory the Advertising and Claims Regulations restrict. And FSSAI's June 2026 notice round explicitly cited "inaccurate vitamin disclosures" and serving sizes as violations, while this product discloses no vitamin quantities at all.

### What the science says

**Biotin for hair in non-deficient people.** The canonical citation is Patel, Swink and Castelo-Soccio, "A Review of the Use of Biotin for Hair Loss," *Skin Appendage Disorders* 2017;3(3):166-169. It found 18 reported cases. **Every single patient who improved had an underlying pathology** causing poor hair growth: inherited or acquired biotin deficiency, brittle nail syndrome, uncombable hair syndrome. The authors conclude there is a lack of sufficient evidence for supplementation in healthy individuals.

Note the marketing spin circulating on affiliate blogs: "a review found 18 studies and in every one participants saw improvement." That inverts the paper's finding by omitting that all 18 were deficiency cases. Worth flagging as a pattern.

**Does oral zinc block DHT?** No human trial evidence. The mechanistic claim traces to a 1988 in-vitro study in the *British Journal of Dermatology*. There is essentially no modern human trial showing oral zinc lowers serum DHT or treats androgenetic alopecia. Counter-signal: zinc is associated with raising testosterone, which raises DHT substrate.

**Grape seed and green tea as "DHT Blocker."** The evidence is in-silico and in-vitro, computational 5-alpha-reductase docking studies. A 10 mg oral dose of a combined botanical extract has no human RCT support for DHT suppression. Naming that ingredient "DHT Blocker" on a label is the least defensible thing on the pack.

**Multivitamins generally.** Loftfield et al., *JAMA Network Open*, June 2024, roughly 390,000 healthy adults over 20-plus years, found **no association between daily multivitamin use and lower all-cause mortality**, nor cancer, heart or cerebrovascular mortality.

**Gummies specifically, which is the strongest technical case.** ConsumerLab's 2017 multivitamin review found gummies were the format most likely to fail testing. Its 2023 round tested 27 multivitamins, roughly 30 percent failed, including four gummy multivitamins, several at nearly double their claimed folate. Root cause, in ConsumerLab's words: ingredients in a gummy degrade faster, so manufacturers add an "overage" to still meet label claim at expiry.

Labdoor found gummies showed larger label-claim variance than standard multivitamins for five of six key vitamins, with **vitamins A and C varying by more than twice as much.** Both A and C are in this product. Across 75 multivitamins, gummy and chewable formats contained 54 percent less vitamins and 70 percent less minerals than standard formats.

Gummies also carry 1 to 2 year shelf life versus 3 to 5 for tablets. This pack declares 18 months, consistent with that.

To be fair, "gummies are always worse" is not true. For vitamin D3 specifically, gummies outperform tablets on bioavailability because the oil and emulsifier matrix helps fat-solubles. The problem is potency accuracy and stability, not absorption.

**The compound problem here:** undisclosed nutrient quantities, plus a format with documented plus-or-minus 100 percent label variance, plus an 18-month shelf life, plus no published COA. The consumer has no way at any point to know what dose they actually took.

### Safety worth knowing

**Biotin interferes with blood tests.** FDA Safety Communication, November 2017, updated November 2019. **One patient taking high-dose biotin died** after falsely low troponin results on a biotin-susceptible assay, a missed heart attack. Interference affects troponin, thyroid panels (can mimic Graves' disease), hCG and others. FDA notes hair and nail supplements can contain biotin up to 650 times the recommended daily intake.

At 30 mcg, this specific product is very unlikely to cause interference. That is the one place where the low dose is protective. But the label carries no warning, and no dose disclosure, so a consumer cannot assess it themselves.

**Vitamin A stacking.** This gummy uses retinyl palmitate, the preformed and toxic form, at 396 mcg. Upper intake limit is 3,000 mcg RAE per day. The failure mode is stacking: a daily multivitamin plus a hair gummy plus a fortified protein bar can compound quickly. Chronic consequences include liver damage, hypercalcemia and teratogenicity. The Indian aggravating factor: a LocalCircles survey of 47,000-plus respondents found **69 percent take nutraceuticals without a doctor's prescription**, and no D2C brand knows what else the customer is stacking.

### Category context

The single best Indian data point is a 2024 study in *Medicine* from Rajagiri Hospital, Kerala, testing 36 popular protein supplements sold in India: **roughly 70 percent were mislabelled on protein content**, with deficits from under 10 percent to over 50 percent, 14 percent contained aflatoxins, 8 percent pesticide residue.

That study covered protein powders, not gummies. Do not extrapolate the 70 percent figure to gummies as fact. Use it as evidence that Indian supplement label claims are unreliable as a category.

**No independent Indian lab testing of hair or multivitamin gummies exists** from Consumer Voice, LocalCircles or any journalist. No investigative piece on Mosaic Wellness was found from The Ken, The Morning Context, Mint or ThePrint.

ASCI's half-yearly report for April to September 2025 found 98 percent of the 6,117 ads it scrutinised required modification, with Personal Care leading in misleading ads and Health and Nutrition accounting for 61 percent of food and beverage cases.

Man Matters carries roughly 2.2 times the negative-review rate of its sister brands, and it is the only one of the three with more 1-star than 2-star reviews. That J-curve is the signature of buyers who expected a specific outcome and did not get it.

The further a reviewer sits from Man Matters' own funnel, the lower the score. Flipkart's main biotin listing shows 3.9 from 31,463 ratings with roughly 16.4 percent one-star, against 8.5 percent on the brand's own feed and a product page whose four default-visible reviews are all five-star, all posted within a three-week window in January 2026.

The dominant complaint is monotonous and always the same: no result, on hair. Sample:

> "No effect even after 2.5 months. Nor am I receiving any symptom of new hair growth. All the tools, i.e. gummies, roller, shampoo, and serum, were recommended to me, but no results." (17 Mar 2026, 1 star)

> "Not as expected result which you show in 3 months and 6 months to cover all head baldness with hair in advertisement and graphical pictures." (21 Dec 2025, 2 stars)

Secondary complaints, in rough order of frequency: delivery delays on a product people treat as scheduled medicine, discount and wallet mechanics (two independent, specific accusations of MRP markup before discount), unreachable customer support, and refund delays.

Praise clusters overwhelmingly on **taste and format**, not efficacy. Amazon's own sentiment tallies show taste winning everywhere and effectiveness at roughly a coin flip. Genuine positive outcome reports cluster far more convincingly on the sleep and magnesium products than on hair.

One gummy-specific quality flag worth noting, from a verified buyer in June 2026 reporting bitter gummies across three consecutive monthly orders and no response to repeated support reports.

## 6. Verdict

**Are the gummies dangerous?** No. At 30 mcg biotin, 2 mg zinc and 396 mcg vitamin A, this is a very low-dose product. The doses are too small to cause the biotin lab-test interference that plagues 5,000 to 10,000 mcg products. The base is pectin and prebiotic fibre. It is, pharmacologically, close to a fortified sweet.

**Are they credible?** Partly. The company is real, well-funded, professionally run, audited by a Big Four affiliate, and near breakeven. The manufacturing entities are real and FSSAI-licensed. The Shilajit line's per-batch NABL testing is genuinely better than category norm.

**Are they likely to work?** For hair, on the published evidence, no. 30 mcg of biotin will do nothing for a person who is not biotin deficient, and the peer-reviewed review that everyone cites says exactly that. The "93% men saw reduced hairfall in 90 days" claim traces to nothing. The zinc-blocks-DHT mechanism has no human trial support. The 10 mg botanical "DHT Blocker" has in-vitro evidence only.

**The single sharpest fact:** a product sold as "Biotin Hair Gummies" at Rs 549 a month, advertised with a 93 percent efficacy stat, contains 30 mcg of biotin, never states that number on its own page, and the most expensive pack in the range has a weaker formula than the cheapest one.

**The fair caveat:** none of this is unique to Man Matters. It is what the Indian nutraceutical regime permits and, given a Rs 3 lakh maximum penalty against a Rs 267 crore ad budget, arguably incentivises. Man Matters is simply the best-funded and most visible example.

## Open questions worth chasing

1. The two "peer-reviewed" Shilajit studies. Not findable in PubMed. Ask the company for DOIs.
2. Whether the "Australian Made Safe Certified" badge is real. Check the MADE SAFE registry directly.
3. Whether any ASCI complaint exists against Mosaic Wellness. The database needs a browser session; it is not queryable over HTTP.
4. CTRI registration status. Search rejected automated queries.
5. Reddit, particularly r/tressless and r/IndianSkincareAddicts. The 30 mcg dosage angle is exactly what that audience would have caught.
6. Whether the batch-lookup Honest Report page actually resolves to a real report. It is client-side rendered and returned an empty body.
