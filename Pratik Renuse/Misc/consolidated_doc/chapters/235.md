Welcome to the wild world of startup life! If you're here, it's likely because you've joined a startup and your background is largely in more mature, established and perhaps even enterprise-level companies or organizations — e.g. Google, Microsoft, Berkshire Hathaway, within defense or healthcare, etc.

Like so many before you, you probably feel as if you're in a "sink or swim" environment, and you're not wrong. Only 4–7% of startups make it to $1M in annual revenue, and even fewer make it to $5M+.

If you can be aware of key operating differences and embody the below principles, you'll be well on your way to less frustration and greater success.

### **TL;DR**

Startup leaders are adaptable, creative and scrappy generalists who are comfortable with ambiguity and unknowns, and who optimize for speed, iteration, building lightweight systems, and avoid hiring "too senior too fast."

### **Operating Principles**

Here are more detailed key comparisons (overly simplified in some places on purpose) that I would love for you to get into your bones as a foundational understanding and operating philosophy:

| **Enterprise / Mature Company Life** | **Startup Life** |
| :-: | :-: |
| **Clear & Abiding Hierarchy** - Explicit swim lanes. Generally only "one job" to do. Many approval layers. Akin to the chain of command within medicine or the Army. | **Leaders Wear Multiple "Hats"** - Overlapping responsibilities/duties exist in the earliest days to ensure coverage. Minimal approval layers. Akin to scrappy and agile Navy Seal or Special Forces teams. |
| **Specialists & Identity** - Leaders are rewarded for depth, track records, functional expertise, results and clear swim lane ownership. Think of a traditional Coach or Manager role, getting results through others. Mindset: "I am valuable because I've seen this before." | **Generalists & Identity** - Startup leaders are rewarded for range, being comfortable with ambiguity, speed, their contributions, problem-solving/being solutions-oriented, and being "in the trenches" with the team. Think "Player-Coach," both guiding and doing mission critical work themselves. Mindset: "I am valuable because I can help figure this out." |
| **Sufficient Resources/Infrastructure** - There are enough people, technical tools and dedicated/shared resources for leaders to lean on and get results through. Mindset: "How do we allocate resources?" | **Resource Scarcity** - There are not enough people, hours in the day, or even funds to be able to do everything. Ruthless prioritization is key. Mindset: "How can we creatively get results without resources?" |
| **Certainty & Evidence** - Through many years of existence, there's an abundance of data and "known paths to success" that support effective decision-making. Emphasis on improvement. Mature companies can have more of a consensus culture. Wrong decisions are expensive. | **Urgency & Constant Unknowns** - Decisions are made on a combination of incomplete data and intuition. Leaders are part of discovering, experimenting with and iterating on strategies to figure out what works. Many decisions are reversible, and speed is mission critical. Alignment > consensus. Slow decisions are expensive. |
| **Longer Time Horizons + Heavyweight Systems** - 1-2 year roadmaps. Heavyweight systems like OKRs and formal PRs are the norm. Structured OKRs are common in more mature companies. | **Regular & Rapid Reorientation + Lightweight Systems** - Shorter time frames & lightweight systems support experimentation. Pivots are very common when greater clarity is gained from experimentation. SMART goals & KPIs are more common. |
| **Performance "Trust"** - Often, managers normally only ask for detailed daily reports when someone is on a Performance Improvement Plan; otherwise, people are generally left to do their jobs and follow the normal communication channels. | **"In the Details"** - Daily standups or huddles (reporting out your daily tasks, blocks and status of project completion) are normal and often necessary to ensure deep alignment, rapid execution and to help unblock team members. |

### **What you can do about it:**

#### **If you're new to your startup:**

  - Be a sponge. Resist the urge to prove yourself right out the gates, and check alllllll of your assumptions at the door. Yes, what you've done in other companies before is helpful to keep in mind, but be deeply committed to figuring out what works in this company.
    There's so much context and nuance you don't yet have on how this org operates, and frankly, things can change on a daily (even hourly) basis. Become a curious, note-taking and pattern recognition machine.

      - Ask to shadow others as much as possible, especially your manager.
          - *See: [Onboarding a new hire](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1FwrvtGTdhCbxparQ3a4y7Z5RidSHXzsZORoGTubcY3U/edit) (2 min) and [Teaching/Training](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1sGF4V0xFWjESabSZElwF3YienjtiLZtp81YTHe-zMpM/edit) (2 min)*
      - Write notes by hand to activate the deep learning part of your brain.
      - What jargon or language does this team use in this industry?
      - How do we talk about who owns what here?
      - What systems already exist? What gaps do I think I see? Where does the nuance lie?
      - Especially in a remote culture, what are people's communication preferences?
      - How do we ruthlessly prioritize amid a sea of things that need doing?

#### **In general for leaders and individual contributors alike:**

  - Unless you're explicitly told otherwise, you as a leader must be a "player-coach."
      - In addition to guiding your team, you must do some degree of hands-on work alongside your team members. (You'll do this until the company has matured enough for you to have a more robust team under you to whom you can more fully delegate the hands-on work.)
      - This is NOT an excuse to micromanage. Work on mastering top-notch delegation and accountability skills to get results *through* your team members, and stay focused on tackling work that truly only you can do.

  - Be part of the solution, don't just point out problems.
      - There's very likely historic or new context that you don't have yet. Ask for it.
      - ["I intend to"](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1Uf5W4C0KG6Ywgsnh0TFlNbZTGymdgeTODAkpBYfj6Bo/edit) can be a powerful framework to use even if you're only 1% confident to help give people something to respond to.

  - Don't assume someone's on it. Make sure they are.
      - Leverage repeat-backs in meetings. "Hey everyone, let's play back our actions out loud to make sure we're on the same page and we didn't miss something? I'm doing X by Z…"
          - *See: [DRIs:  Directly-Responsible Individuals](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1I4jBy1XryEdknFoDCMUY93PEn0rR-oOOx6yiLSiaa38/edit) (3 min) and [Agreement-Tracker:  why you need one](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1QzGvP6MrmlD6YVilTiNjEsPgRRvjzvWrqGXKSz_c68k/edit) (3 min)*

  - If you make a mistake, flag it quickly to someone who can help so it can be addressed and then do your best to learn from what happened so you can prevent it from happening again in the future.

  - When someone who reports to you is not performing well, check yourself first.
      - Anchor to the behavior (not the person). What are they doing that needs improvement?
      - Have you checked in with them with genuine curiosity about what's going on to ensure you understand and you're not just assuming you know what's happening?
      - Have you set clear expectations?
      - Have you provided them with the training and context they need to be successful?
