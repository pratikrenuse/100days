## Project doc: CLEAR_UI_Reference.md (uploaded 2026-05-23)

# C.L.E.A.R. UI Principles (quick reference)

Apply all five pillars to any screen, then score each 0 to 5 (25 total). Fix the lowest first. It's a loop: after a change, re-check earlier pillars.

**C, Copywriting.** Make the benefit obvious in 1 to 2 seconds (WIIFM). Cut any sentence that breaks nothing if removed. Sound like a person, not a brochure. Specific, active verbs; reassure near friction.

**L, Layout.** Use Gestalt grouping: same role = same look (Similarity); tight spacing within groups, loose between them (Proximity); remove meaningless styling (Simplicity); commit to a 4px grid and align edges (Alignment); group with cards/sections (Common Region); one clear top-left-to-bottom scan path (Continuity).

**E, Emphasis.** Tune six dials: Size, Color, Space, Placement, Visualization, Motion. Lead with 1 to 2; let the rest support. Squint Test: the key element must dominate even when blurred. If everything shouts, nothing stands out.

**A, Accessibility.** Design for permanent, temporary, and situational limits. Every screen: visible without searching, operable without precision, actionable without guessing. Big tap targets (Fitt's Law), 4.5:1+ text contrast, never color alone, prevent errors (disable invalid, allow undo, confirm irreversible).

**R, Reward.** Deliver an emotional outcome matched to the moment: Control (safety, certainty, undo), Competence (progress, "done" states, streaks), or Recognition (badges, roles, reactions). The screen must answer at least one of "Am I safe? Did I do well? Am I recognized?" Keep rewards proportional.

**Copy guardrails:** no hype, no emojis, no em dashes, direct and clear.

#### 6. C.L.E.A.R. UI framework, apply before shipping any page

Walk all five pillars in order, score each 0-5 (25 total), fix the lowest first, then re-check the earlier pillars (it is a loop). Full detail lives in `CLEAR_UI_Reference.md` in this repo.

- **C, Copywriting.** Make the benefit obvious in 1-2 seconds (WIIFM). Cut any sentence that breaks nothing if removed (Barstool Test). Every button is verb + object ("Send invoice", "Download report"), never "Submit", "Click here", "Learn more", "OK", "Continue", "Next". Apply the **Copy Swap Test**: if another company could use the same words, it is generic and must be rewritten. No repetition between adjacent sections.
- **L, Layout.** 6 Gestalt rules (Similarity, Proximity, Simplicity, Alignment, Common Region, Continuity). Commit to a **4px grid** and align edges. Tight spacing within groups, loose between. Subtle background tints beat hairline borders. Start with too much padding and pull back.
- **E, Emphasis.** Six dials: Size, Color, Space, Placement, Visualization, Motion. Lead with 1-2, let the rest support. **Squint Test:** blur the page and ONE element must dominate. Highlights in body must be rare and earned, not on every key phrase.
- **A, Accessibility.** Visible without searching (primary action above the fold), operable without precision (44px+ touch targets, 8px+ spacing, Fitt's Law), actionable without guessing (labels say what happens). **4.5:1 text contrast minimum**, never color alone, prevent errors (disable invalid, allow undo, confirm irreversible).
- **R, Reward.** Deliver an emotional outcome matched to the moment: **Control** (safety, certainty, undo), **Competence** (progress, "done" states, streaks), or **Recognition** (badges, roles, reactions). Never silent successes. Be specific ("Saved. Live in 30 seconds", not "Saved").

**Copy guardrails:** no hype, no emojis, no em dashes. Direct and clear.

**Each page needs:** one unmissable primary action, benefit-led copy, Gestalt grouping, accessible contrast and tap targets, and a fitting emotional payoff.

### taste.md: how Pratik writes

Use this for every email, message, reply, pitch or post written as Pratik. The goal: it should read like he typed it himself, not like an AI or a marketer.

#### How he actually talks

- Plain, everyday words. Says "very good fit", "it seems like", "entirely on my own", "just in the last 15 days".

  - "With a $2,500 budget, that discipline matters more than anything."
  - "Every wasted click on a retail searcher is money not spent on a coach."
  - "That's the job here."

### Futures Brain

The engine behind the daily future-insight posts. Not a news folder. A map of where the world is heading in 10 to 20 years, and the specific, mostly unknown players who will quietly decide it.

#### The one idea

Organise everything by **thesis**, not by topic. A topic is a pile of news. A thesis is a bet about the future, and a bet is something new information can confirm, sharpen, or break.

The filter that keeps this alive: a new item only earns a place here if it **moves a thesis** and can answer two questions, *what does this imply in 10 to 20 years*, and *which hidden player does it point to*. If it can't, it's noise. Drop it.

#### How it's organised

- **Theses/** the spine. A few master arcs about the future, each a living note. This is the memory map.
- **Players/** the gold. The mostly unknown companies and people who own a chokepoint (think SK Hynix, not "memory"). Each links to the thesis it proves.
- **Sources/** the registry of future thinkers and feeds worth reading. Seeded with my picks, ready for yours.
- **Signals/** the inbox. Raw incoming items wait here until they get filed under a thesis or thrown out.
- **Posts/** output and the idea backlog. Published pieces plus the queue.
- **Style Guide.md** the locked voice, format, and rules every post must clear.

#### How a post gets made

1. Scan Sources and Signals for the item that best advances a thesis.
2. Pull the **hidden player** it points to (the unknown name is the hook, the theme is just setup).
3. Write the **10 to 20 year implication** in the locked voice.
4. File the post, update the thesis note, and drop the awareness score on anything the crowd is starting to notice.

#### The awareness rule (the edge)

Every thesis and player carries an **awareness score**: obscure, building, or mainstream. The engine fishes from the high-importance, low-awareness corner. The day the crowd catches on (memory stops being a secret), that node retires from the hook pool and we move to the next unknown. This is what keeps us early instead of trend-chasing.

#### Scope

Frontier tech is the centre of gravity (AI, compute, energy, robotics, biology, space). Anything else can earn a place, as long as it genuinely reshapes the next 10 to 20 years.

### Style Guide (locked)

The voice and rules every post must clear. Built and tested across the June 2026 session, sharpened on 2026-06-30 into the punchier deepdive format. Inspired by Jayant Mundra's LinkedIn deep dives, in our own words.

Start with [[00 The Master Template]]. It is the single source of truth: the full pattern, the beat-by-beat skeleton, and the **two gold-standard posts it was built from, GE Vernova (power) and Astera Labs (bandwidth)**, both embedded in full. Those two share one identical eight-beat pattern in completely different words: that is the standard now. This guide and [[Post Format (LinkedIn Deepdive)]] hold the same rules in more detail, but note both were written for the older, longer deepdive cut. Where they conflict with the Master Template (the newer, tighter, warmer, jargon-free voice aimed at a normal reader, ~2,000 to 2,200 characters), the Master Template wins.

#### The voice

Write like a real person who went down a rabbit hole and cannot wait to tell a smart friend what they found. Curious, informal, genuinely startled. Fast. Confident, but honest about what would prove the take wrong.

Open on a real reaction in your own words, paired with a hard, surprising, dated fact. A punchy conversational hook is wanted, but it has to be fresh. Do not reach for a stock exclamation you have used before, and do not borrow one from an example post. The surprise has to be real, never performed. Then get straight into the story.

What kills the "AI smell":
- Ground everything in concrete, slightly obscure specifics (the real company, the real number with its date, the actual mechanism). Specificity is what makes it human.
- Avoid tidy metaphors stacked on tidy metaphors, and avoid fortune-cookie aphorisms in every paragraph. One earned line at the end is plenty.
- Vary sentence length. Let some sentences just be ordinary.
- Drop announced transitions like "here's the part worth sitting with" if they start to feel like scaffolding.

#### Hard rules

- **No em dashes. Ever.** Use commas, colons, periods, or parentheses.
- **Flow, don't announce.** Do not open every `..` section with a stock announcement ("here's the thing", "that's when it hit me", "here's where it gets strange", "the thought I can't shake", "now here's the wild part", "and honestly"). One such signal per post at most. Most sections should open on the substance and connect to the line before, as a consequence ("So..."), a contrast ("But..."), or a continuation, so the post reads as one flowing article, not a stack of announcements. A reaction inside a beat ("Cables!") is fine; an announcement that opens a beat is the problem.
- Genuine conversational shock is fine ("My god", "Wild", "this broke my brain"). Marketing-hype adjectives are not ("stunning", "mind-blowing", "game-changer", "revolutionary", "worship"). The test: a startled friend would say it, an ad would not.
- No emojis.
- Every claim is dated and sourced in the relevant Player or Thesis note, so facts age honestly.
- The hook is the **hidden player** or the unseen cause, not the theme. People know "memory matters". Almost nobody knows SK Hynix. Lead with the unknown name.
- Every post must end on a concrete 10 and 20 year takeaway tied to a pattern. That payoff is the whole point of the post, never optional, never a year-stamped prediction.
- The takeaway must be a specific claim, not a mood. Name what gets bought, what becomes scarce, who collects the money, or a number that crosses a threshold. "We stop buying compute and start buying bandwidth" is a claim. "Funny where the money hides" is filler.
- Teach one real mechanism with concrete, dated numbers. If the post does not leave the reader knowing *how* something works that they did not know before, it is not finished.
- Invent your own connective tissue. Never reuse the signature transitions of an example post ("First up", "delicious irony", "here is the part I keep thinking about", "this is what X actually does"). If a line could be pasted into another post unchanged, it is too generic. (A short "No?" close is sanctioned, Jayant-style, but do not end every single post identically.)
- Kill crutch phrases. If "bottleneck", "one floor below", "the rung", or any pet phrase recurs across posts, find a fresh, specific way to say the real thing.
- Never command the reader or write stage directions. Banned, and anything like them: "Start here", "Think about this", "Think about what that means", "Picture this", "Imagine", "Consider", "Let me explain", "Stay with me", "Now watch". Do not tell the reader to do anything or narrate how to read. Just say it straight and let the facts pull them. First-person reactions ("the part that got me", "my jaw dropped") are fine; reader-commands are not.
- Sanity-check every sentence. No absurd hyperbole or outlier claims a smart reader would call out as nonsense ("raise a hundred billion dollars in a weekend"). If a line is not literally defensible, rewrite it true: "AI companies are raising tens of billions at a time." Exaggeration that breaks under one second of thought kills trust in everything else.

#### What kills a post dead (never write like this)
We studied a real post that got zero engagement, zero likes, to lock in the failure mode. Never do any of these. If a draft has any of it, cut it entirely.

- **It's about you, not about what the reader learns.** The dead post was a feelings-journey about the writer's own integrity ("I pride myself...", "the most uncomfortable deliverable of my career", "the most accurate thing I've produced in years"). The reader finished having learned nothing they could repeat. Our "I" is only a guide pointing at an external discovery, never the subject. Using "I" and "you" is good; making the post about you is fatal.
- **Abstraction with no specifics.** "a massive IT firm", "the entire tech sector", "the illusion of control". No names, no real facts, nothing to picture. Always name the concrete company or thing and teach dated facts.
- **Humble-bragging and performed thoughtfulness.** The "look how honest and brave I am" arc reads as performance and gets scrolled past. Deliver the goods, do not preen.
- **A vague big-concept hook.** "Every 5-year plan today is guesswork. Welcome to the illusion of control." That is a thesis statement, not a scroll-stopper. Open on one concrete, surprising fact.
- **Ending on helplessness or a non-conclusion.** The dead post's whole point was "this is unknowable, so I wrote a disclaimer." That deflates the reader. We end on a confident, concrete forecast that leaves them feeling smarter and ahead.
- **Engagement-bait aimed at a narrow clique.** "Modelers, analysts, strategy folks, what do you actually do?" is homework for a tiny group. If we ask anything, it is one easy, universal question anyone can answer.
- **Jargon with no payoff** (DCF, mean-revert, back-tested, margin of safety) that teaches a normal reader nothing.

The single test: if the reader cannot repeat one concrete, surprising thing they just learned, the post is dead. Cut it and start again from a real fact.

#### The standing standard (write every post this way)
The canonical template is [[00 The Master Template]], built from **two gold-standard posts**: `Posts/Format Examples/GE Vernova (creator standard).md` and `Posts/Format Examples/Astera Labs (creator standard).md`. Both are reference-grade, study both. They run the same eight beats (personal hook, reveal + reaction, the simple explanation, the hidden player once, the data with a kicker, the realization, a concrete forecast, the today/tomorrow shift close) in different words. Every post follows the pattern:
- **Fixed beats, fresh words.** The structure repeats; the exact phrases must not. Reword the personal investigation beat ("I had to know how that's even possible", "I didn't believe it at first"), the realization beat ("that's when it clicked", "that's when it hit me"), the wrap-up beat ("so here's where I land", "so here's the bet I'd make"), and especially the close, every single time. The two templates use different words for the same beats on purpose: copy the skeleton, not the sentences. The close must vary and must NEVER default to "No?" on every post; a declarative landing or a fresh question both work.
- **Vary the vocabulary too, inside a post and across posts.** Do not lean on one word. The Astera post must not say "wiring" six times; reach for the synonyms (cables, links, connections, switches, the parts in between). Across posts, change the texture words so no two read like the same machine wrote them. Same voice, different words.
- **Jayant layout:** heavy white space, one or two sentence lines, "-" bullets, ".." section breaks, short curiosity transitions.
- **Slippery slide:** every line earns the next.
- **Human and personal:** "I" and "you" throughout, with genuine curiosity and reaction ("I went down a rabbit hole on why, and it changed how I think about the AI race"). Do NOT manufacture a personal credential or a fake "I assumed X, I was wrong" humility beat, and never bolt on an identity claim the reader does not need ("I build software for a living, so..."). Those read as performance and kill the post. Personal means genuine, not performed.
- **Investor-grade numbers:** weave in the figures investors, VCs and analysts care about, capex, growth %, valuations, multiples, distributed with white space, never dumped. Examples: "$725 billion of AI capex this year, up 77%"; "GE Vernova spun out at $115, now near $1,100, nearly 10x".
- **Say it out loud.** Write it the way you'd explain it to one smart friend across a table ("Today? It's near 1,100" beats "it now trades near 1,100"). If a line isn't how you'd actually say it, rewrite it.
- **Keep it under the cap.** Always check the character count; LinkedIn cuts off at 3,000, and the heavy white space adds up. Aim ~2,400 to 2,800. A tighter ~2,000-character cut also works when you want it punchier: keep the full story spine and compress the forecast section (drop the bullets, run the fusion and space beats as one short paragraph).
- **One connected story.** This is the whole thing. The post must read as a single narrative that builds: a small contradiction or mystery, an investigation, a realization, then what it means. Each beat flows into the next. Weave every number INTO a story beat, never as a standalone stat. The moment it reads like a list of impressive figures, the story is dead.
- **Logical order, each fact once.** Claim (the hook), then why it's true (the mechanism), then the proof (the player, the scarcity, the numbers). Do not state the punchline, then the scarcity, then circle back to explain it. That out-of-order repetition (saying "it's a gas turbine", then "you can't buy a turbine", then "here's why a turbine matters") is the most common way the story stops connecting. Also set up every concept before you lean on it: if the forecast says "copper gives up", the mechanism must have already told the reader the cables are copper. A payoff that references something you never introduced reads as disconnected. Read it back line by line and make sure each one is earned by the one before it.
- **Introduce the hidden player once, in the right place.** Reveal who it is, why it matters, and its numbers in one clean moment, at the point the story needs it. Never name it early as a throwaway, then re-explain it later. That breaks the dots.
- **Land on the core takeaway, not the tangent.** The speculative or futuristic stuff (space, fusion, rockets) is a short detour in the middle, never the closing emphasis. The ending returns to the one point the whole post was about. ("It started with a contradiction. The four biggest tech firms will spend 725 billion on AI this year. And yet you still can't buy a turbine before 2029." is story. A bullet list of capex, valuations, and multiples is not.)
- **One single takeaway, plus a REQUIRED 10 and 20 year forecast.** Every post must spell out where this goes by roughly 2030 and 2040, grounded in dated numbers and interlinking other players from the brain (GE Vernova lands on fusion and space; Astera lands on optics and buying bandwidth). It is never optional; it is how the post earns its weight and its future angle. Keep every claim sanity-checked, no em dashes, no reader-commands.

#### The format (LinkedIn deepdive layout)

- Heavy white space, Jayant-style. Almost every line is its own paragraph (one or two sentences, never more), with a blank line between. No dense blocks.
- `..` on its own line marks the bigger turns between sections.
- Use `-` bullets liberally: for the punchy dated facts, and also for a run of short rhetorical questions ("- Why does Goa feel more expensive than Da Nang?").
- Short standalone transition lines that build curiosity ("But this gets wild.", "And that is what flipped it for me.", "Why is everyone suddenly serious?"). These are statements, not reader-commands, so they are fine.
- A strong first line that survives the "see more" fold on its own.
- Rhetorical question-and-answer is the signature move for delivering numbers ("Investment? X. Outcome? Y."). Use it two or three times, not ten.
- Length around 300 to 400 words, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 characters, always comfortably under LinkedIn's 3,000 cap (check the count). Substantial enough to earn dwell time (see [[LinkedIn Creator Playbook]]), but shorter and simpler beats long and dense.

#### The shape (not a script)

**One thesis x one hidden player x one 10-to-20-year implication.** Follow the arc of functions in [[Post Format (LinkedIn Deepdive)]], never a fixed set of phrases:

Reorder the reader's world with a surprising dated fact. Set the stakes and name the hidden player. Show the mechanism in concrete numbers, and spend the most words here. Drop a short dated evidence list. Turn on one non-obvious observation specific to this story (the shift). Then add the 10 and 20 year outlook: using our own interlinked notes, sketch where this goes in roughly 10 and 20 years, grounded in real forecasts, with projections clearly labeled as bets. This interlinked outlook is the payoff only our brain can deliver.

Write every transition fresh. Reorder or drop a beat when the story wants it. Two posts should never feel like the same fill-in-the-blanks with the nouns swapped.

#### Pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Opens on a genuine reaction plus a surprising dated fact, hidden player not theme
- [ ] Follows the arc (functions, not fixed phrases), with `..` between turns
- [ ] Teaches one real mechanism with hard, dated numbers
- [ ] Every number is in the Player/Thesis note with a date and source
- [ ] Zero em dashes
- [ ] Genuine shock, no hype adjectives, no emojis
- [ ] No borrowed phrasing or scaffolding; swapping the company name out would not make it read as generic
- [ ] Ends on a concrete 10 and 20 year takeaway stated as a specific claim, not a restatement
- [ ] Around 300 to 400 words, comfortably under 3,000 characters (check the count)

### 01 New Story Pipeline (standing procedure)

This is the locked, repeat-every-time workflow. When a new article or story is chosen (from the Futures Brain or the Trend Scout), produce two things from it, in this order, and save them together. Do not ask how to do this each time. It is all here.

**Input:** one story (a hidden player + the thesis it proves).
**Output, per story, into `Content Engine/Ready to Ship/<Story Name>/`:**
1. `LinkedIn.md` -- the LinkedIn post.
2. `HeyGen script.md` -- the video script (this is what Pratik uploads to HeyGen, aka "Haines").
3. `image` -- one concept image built to the locked art direction in `Templates/Image Style.md` (dark engraved-gold, one honest idea, no gimmicks, no stats). The GE piece is the gold standard.

#### The two steps

**Step 1 -- LinkedIn post.** Write it using `Futures Brain/Posts/00 The Master Template.md` (the source of truth). SHORT and viral: ~1,000 to 1,700 characters, a scroll-stopping hook in the first two lines, ONE idea, at most 1 to 2 numbers, and an ending question that invites comments. Long data-dense posts died on LinkedIn; short and clear wins.

**Step 2 -- HeyGen script.** Adapt the SAME story into a spoken script using `Content Engine/Templates/Video Script (HeyGen).md`. The file is the script and nothing else. Default ~60s; note a 45s and 40s trim only if asked.

**Step 3 -- Concept image.** Build one image to `Content Engine/Templates/Image Style.md`: dark background, engraved metallic gold, ONE honest idea (usually the grand AI world depending on one small boring thing), a short witty caption, the Pratik Renuse wordmark. No gimmicky object-morphs, no stats, no "essay" chrome. Built in code (Pillow), not AI-generated. Reference recipe: `Templates/image-scripts/GE Vernova - render.py`.

All three draw from the same facts. Assemble the substance once, then shape it three ways.

---

#### The locked patterns (this is what "don't make me repeat myself" means)

Every post and every script obeys all of these. They are already baked into the two templates; this is the short, non-negotiable summary so nothing gets lost.

**Write for a normal person who knows nothing about AI.** Zero jargon. Banned words: supercomputer, cluster, bandwidth, silicon, terabytes, interconnect, optics, retimer. If a topic is abstract, open it with one everyday analogy (an AI is like a brain, the magic is how fast the pieces talk). Plain, simple language, the way you'd explain it to a friend.

**Be human. Use the reaction elements.** This is the one that keeps getting asked for, so it is locked: sprinkle real, everyday reactions where the surprises land. "Yeah, a gas turbine.", "Cables!", "But get this.", "And you know what?", "Now here's the wild part.", "you basically can't buy one.", "Insane, right?", "Wild, right?" A few, at the genuine surprises, not on every line. Video should be even warmer than the post.

**Fixed beats, fresh words. Never reuse a signature line.** The structure repeats every time; the exact sentences never do. Rotate the hook, the reaction, the realization, the forecast opener, and the close on every single piece. Concretely: GE closes with "So here's my bet," so Astera must NOT; it closes "So here's where it's heading." A viewer or reader who sees two pieces should never feel it's a template with the nouns swapped.

**Flow, don't announce.** Don't open every section with a stock announcement ("here's the thing", "that's when it hit me", "here's where it gets strange", "the thought I can't shake"). One signal per piece at most; let the other sections flow from the line before, as a consequence or a continuation. Rotating one stock announcement into a fresher one is still the same crutch. The goal is one connected article, not a stack of "and now, this!" openers. A reaction inside a beat is fine; an announcement that opens a beat is not.

**One idea, barely any data (LinkedIn).** Keep at most 1 to 2 numbers in the whole post, woven in plainly. Cut the stat runs, bullet lists, and mechanism detail. Clarity and a single surprising point beat a full briefing. Save the deep data for a carousel or the video.

**Always end on a question.** Comments are worth ~15x a like on LinkedIn. Close every post with one easy, open question anyone can answer in a line.

**Say each point once.** No dragging one idea across two or three restated lines. Make it, move on.

**Concrete forecast, never vague futurism.** Ground the future in a real 2030 number or a named brain player (Ayar Labs, fusion, small nuclear). "You'll buy speed by the second" means nothing and gets cut.

**The shift close (the thesis, in plain words).** End on: today the winner is X, tomorrow it'll be Y, because [the timeless pattern]. That is the "bottleneck slides down the stack" idea said simply.

**Never make it about the writer.** No fake credentials, no humble-brags ("I build software for a living, so..."). "I" is only a guide pointing at the discovery.

**No em dashes. No emojis. No reader-commands** ("Start here", "Think about", "Picture this").

**Sanity-check every claim.** Nothing that breaks under a second of thought.

---

#### Channel-specific reminders

**LinkedIn (`00 The Master Template.md`):** eight beats, `..` between turns, `-` bullets for the dated facts, heavy white space, ~2,000 to 2,200 characters, `source` field set to the project when the signup strip is used.

**HeyGen (`Video Script (HeyGen).md`):** the file is a title plus the spoken script, nothing else. No on-screen overlays, no delivery notes, no runtime block. HeyGen reads every word aloud, so pure spoken words only, no brackets. Numbers spoken naturally ("nearly ten times", not "10x"). Default ~60s, warmer than the post.

---

#### Where it runs
- **Weekly, automatically:** the `futures-brain-weekly` scheduled task runs this pipeline on the week's best stories after it updates the brain.
- **On demand:** when a Trend Scout item or any article is picked, run this same pipeline on it.

Both paths produce the same two files per story in `Ready to Ship/<Story Name>/`. Nothing auto-publishes; Pratik reviews and posts.

### C.L.E.A.R. UI Framework

Most pages that look polished but underperform are failing one or more of five pillars: **Copywriting, Layout, Emphasis, Accessibility, Reward**. This skill encodes each as a specific diagnostic and a specific fix.

The order matters. Apply them in sequence. Earlier failures cascade: ugly type with great copy is recoverable; beautiful design with generic copy is not.

#### The five pillars in 30 seconds

1. **Copywriting**, Words come first. If users can't tell what your button does or what your page is about, design is irrelevant. Buttons are `verb + object`. Hero answers "what's in it for me" in one sentence.
2. **Layout**, Gestalt principles govern how the brain groups information. Proximity, common region, alignment do the structural work that borders should not.
3. **Emphasis**, Pick ONE primary action per section. Use the right dial (size, color, placement, space, visualization, motion) to make it pop. The Squint Test verifies.
4. **Accessibility**, Visible without searching, Operable without precision, Actionable without guessing. 44px+ targets, real labels, contrast that survives sunlight.
5. **Reward**, Every completed action needs a clear, proportionate signal. Pick the right reward from the Trifecta: Control, Competence, or Recognition.

#### C, Copywriting

The first thing users do on a page is read. Generic words kill conversions before design has a chance to work.

##### The three copy mistakes

1. **Long copy.** Three lines of prose in a card and people skip the entire card. The web is scanned, not read.
2. **Generic copy.** "Power your business." "Get started today." "Streamline your workflow." Works for anyone, sells nothing.
3. **Unnecessary duplicates.** Repeating the value prop in hero + sub + button + bullet just adds noise.

##### The four practical fixes

1. **Emphasize benefits, not features.** "Get back 2 hours a week" beats "AI-powered scheduling." Lead with what changes for the user.
2. **Reassure where there's friction.** "You can change this later." "No credit card needed." "Cancel anytime." A single reassurance unlocks more conversions than a redesign.
3. **Use specific verbs and concrete outcomes.** Every button is `verb + object`. "Send invoice," "Download report," "Continue to payment." Real evidence: changing "Submit" to "Send invoice" lifted CTR 18% in one documented case.
4. **Talk like a real person.** Read the copy out loud. If you naturally rephrase as you read, the written version is wrong.

##### The Copy Swap Test (the diagnostic)

Hide your logo and visuals. Read the copy aloud. Ask: **could another company use these exact words on their site?**

If yes, every line that fails is generic copy. Rewrite.

##### Button copy rules (non-negotiable)

- **Never use:** "Submit," "Click here," "Learn more," "Next," "Continue," "OK." These are placeholder words.
- **Always use:** `Verb + Object` where possible. "Send invoice." "Download report." "Add to cart." "Reserve a seat."
- **For destructive actions:** be explicit. "Delete account" not "Confirm." Match button label to consequence.
- **For low-friction CTAs:** consider benefit framing. "Start my free trial" beats "Sign up." "See my results" beats "Continue."
- **Length:** 2-5 words. Past 5 and the button stops feeling like an action.

##### Microcopy beyond buttons

- **Form fields:** label what the field is FOR, not just what it IS. "Work email (we'll send your receipts here)" beats "Email."
- **Error messages:** never blame the user. "We didn't recognize that email" beats "Invalid input."
- **Empty states:** explain what would be here, and how to fill it. Empty + silent is the worst combination.
- **Loading states:** if it takes >1 second, tell the user what's happening. "Saving your draft" beats a spinner.

#### L, Layout

Gestalt principles aren't optional folklore. They're how the visual cortex literally groups what it sees. Violate them and the brain works harder to understand the page, which means more bounces.

##### The six Gestalt principles every UI uses

1. **Proximity**, Elements close together are perceived as a group. The strongest grouping principle, usually overpowering color or shape. Whitespace is the tool here, not borders.
2. **Common Region**, Elements inside the same container (card, panel, box) are read as one unit. Material Design cards are pure common-region.
3. **Alignment**, Elements on a shared axis feel related and intentional. Misalignment looks like a bug even when functionally nothing's wrong.
4. **Similarity**, Same color, shape, or size signals same kind of thing. All buttons must look like buttons.
5. **Continuity**, The eye follows lines and curves. Arranged elements feel connected.
6. **Simplicity (Prägnanz)**, The brain prefers the simplest interpretation. Don't make it work harder than it has to.

##### Practical application

- **For grouping form fields:** use proximity (whitespace between groups) first, common region (subtle background) second, borders only as last resort.
- **For showing relationships:** stack aligned items; the alignment IS the relationship.
- **For separating sections:** lots of whitespace beats a divider line.
- **For card grids:** common region (the card itself) handles grouping; don't add internal borders.

##### The three layout mistakes

1. **Sloppy spacing.** Spacing is inconsistent or missing. **Fix:** start with *too much* padding (2-2.5rem in cards, 6-8rem section padding) and pull back. Almost every cramped design I've audited started with "good enough" padding that wasn't.
2. **Border bloat.** Drawing boxes around everything because you don't trust whitespace. **Fix:** define areas with subtle background tints (`#EEE8E4` on `#F6F2F1` for example, a 6% shift), not hairlines. Use borders sparingly, for actual separation.
3. **Content cramming.** Squeezing too much info into one screen. **Fix:** remove elements (see Copywriting), use progressive disclosure (hide secondary info behind "Show more"), split into steps.

##### Padding defaults to start from

- Cards holding prose: **2-2.5rem** all sides
- Form panels: **2-2.5rem**
- Section vertical padding: **4-8rem** desktop, **2.5-3.5rem** mobile
- Section horizontal: **4rem** desktop, **1.25rem** mobile
- Button: **0.8-1rem** vertical, **1.5-2.2rem** horizontal

#### E, Emphasis

Every section should have **exactly one primary thing**. If three things compete for attention, the user has to choose, which means thinking, which means leaving.

##### The six emphasis dials

To make one thing stand out, turn ONE of these dials up while keeping the others quiet:

1. **Size**, Bigger = more important. Don't be subtle; a 2px bump is not emphasis. For primary actions, go 1.3-1.5x.
2. **Color**, Saturated color in a muted page pulls the eye. Reserve your accent color (e.g., the gold) for ONE job per section.
3. **Placement**, Top-left and center get seen first in left-reading languages. F-pattern (for content-heavy) and Z-pattern (for hero/landing) are the default scan paths.
4. **Space**, Surrounding whitespace amplifies importance more than size does. A small button alone in a quiet area beats a huge button in a cluttered grid.
5. **Visualization**, Turn a number into a chart, a comparison into a diagram, a process into steps. Visual representations carry more weight than text of the same data.
6. **Motion**, A single animated element is the strongest attention grabber. Which is why it should be used almost never. Reserve for the one action you really need seen.

##### Choose the right dial

| Goal                                        | Use                  |
|---------------------------------------------|----------------------|
| Make the primary CTA unmissable             | Size + Color         |
| Highlight a specific stat or quote          | Space + Size         |
| Draw the eye through a sequence             | Placement + Continuity |
| Communicate data relationships              | Visualization        |
| Pull attention back from anywhere on page   | Motion (sparingly)   |

##### The Foggy Glasses Test (the diagnostic)

Squint at your design until detail blurs out. What's left is contrast, size, position, spacing. **The thing that stands out is what the brain will see first.**

If that's not the thing you wanted users to see, your hierarchy is wrong.

A practical alternative: load the page, then walk 6 feet away and look. Or use Polypane's far-sightedness emulator. Or screenshot and apply a strong Gaussian blur.

##### The three emphasis mistakes

1. **Wrong Dial**, Turned up Size when the page needed Space. Or Color when it needed Placement. Each dial does a different job; pick the one that matches the goal.
2. **Weak Dial**, Emphasis exists but it's so subtle nobody sees it. A 16px → 18px bump is not emphasis. The bump has to be obvious.
3. **Screaming Dial**, Emphasis on everything (every button is gold, every heading is huge, three things animate). Emphasis on everything means emphasis on nothing.

##### The one-primary-action rule

In every distinct section of your page, ONE thing should be the obvious next action. Secondary actions exist but visibly stand down: less color, less weight, less size. Tertiary actions are text links, not buttons.

If you can't pick which action is primary, the problem is upstream: the page is trying to do too much at once.

#### A, Accessibility

Most accessibility wins are usability wins for everyone. Reframe accessibility from "compliance checkbox" to "design that works for real people in real conditions."

##### The eight accessibility mistakes

1. **Tiny targets**, buttons under 44px square miss frequently, especially on mobile.
2. **Crowded areas**, interactive elements packed together cause mis-clicks; users hit the wrong button.
3. **Low-contrast text**, anything under 7:1 fatigues the eye after a paragraph; under 4.5:1 fails accessibility.
4. **Icons only, no labels**, a hamburger icon is recognized; a custom icon never is. Always pair with text.
5. **Key actions hidden**, primary CTA below the fold, or behind a hover state, or only accessible via gesture.
6. **Color-only meaning**, "click the red one" fails for color-blind users (8% of men). Use color PLUS shape, label, or position.
7. **Multiple inconsistent patterns**, one screen uses cards, the next uses lists, the next uses tiles. Pick one pattern per content type.
8. **Assumed knowledge**, using jargon, internal terms, or features without explanation.

##### The three accessibility design principles

These are the cleanest summary of accessibility I've seen. Memorize as **Find / Touch / Trust**:

###### Visible without searching (Find)

Can the user see the main action without digging, scrolling, or guessing? Is it above the fold?

- Hero CTA visible on initial viewport, every viewport size.
- Primary actions in expected locations (top-right for account, top-center for nav, center for hero CTA, bottom-right for floating action).
- If you have to explain where to find something, it's hidden.

###### Operable without precision (Touch)

Can users tap/click/select actions even with reduced capability? Thumbs, motion impairment, fatigue, distraction?

- **Targets: 44×44px minimum.** WCAG 2.2 sets the AA floor at 24×24, AAA at 44×44; iOS Human Interface Guidelines and Apple recommend 44pt; Material Design recommends 48dp.
- **Spacing: 8-10px minimum between adjacent targets.** Stops mis-taps.
- **Hit area > visual area.** A 28px-tall button can be tappable across 44px if you add invisible padding.
- **Keyboard-accessible.** Every interactive element reachable by Tab; visible focus state for every focusable element.
- **No precision-required gestures.** Hover-only menus, double-taps, long-presses all exclude users.

###### Actionable without guessing (Trust)

Do actions look like actions? Self-explanatory? Do users have to guess what will happen?

- **Buttons look like buttons.** Boxes, with text inside, that look pressable. Not just colored text.
- **Labels describe the outcome.** "Send invoice" tells you what will happen. "Confirm" does not.
- **Affordances are consistent.** Links underlined, buttons filled or outlined, toggles look toggleable.
- **No fake elements.** Don't style non-clickable text to look clickable, or vice versa.

##### Contrast quick reference

For body text on backgrounds:

- AAA (the target): 7:1 minimum
- AA (the floor): 4.5:1 minimum
- Light text on dark: bump up by ~20% over light-on-dark equivalents
- See the readable-web skill for specific color pair tables

##### Mobile-specific rules

- Touch targets: 44px+ (iOS) or 48dp+ (Material)
- Bottom-edge actions: 12mm+ (≈46px), since thumbs are clumsier here
- Top-edge actions: 11mm+ (≈42px), still usable but harder to reach
- Avoid critical actions in the dead zone (top corners on large phones)

#### R, Reward

The pillar most often missed in B2B/serious software design. After a user completes an action, what do they get?

##### The core insight

Every interaction needs a clear positive signal at completion. Silence or ambiguity creates anxiety: "did that work?" The brain wants confirmation. When it doesn't get one, the user feels uncertain even when the task succeeded.

##### The Reward Trifecta

Three kinds of payoff users actually want. Different products need different mixes:

1. **Control**, Feeling safe, certain, in charge. "Saved." "You can undo this." "We won't share your email." "Your data is encrypted." **Use for:** financial apps, security flows, anything where the user fears losing something. Control rewards reduce anxiety.

2. **Competence**, Feeling improvement, forward motion, getting better. Progress bars, "you've completed 3 of 5," streaks, XP, levels, "your fastest time yet." **Use for:** learning apps, fitness, productivity, gamified onboarding. Competence rewards drive return visits.

3. **Recognition**, Feeling seen, valued, belonging. "Welcome back, Pratik." "You're our 47th member." "Your post got 12 replies." "@John mentioned you." **Use for:** social products, community features, anything multi-user. Recognition rewards build emotional attachment.

##### The three reward mistakes

1. **Wrong Reward**, Delivering a payoff the user didn't want in that moment. Confetti for paying a medical bill (somber moment, needs Control reward). A "great job!" after a serious transaction (needs a precise confirmation, not praise).

2. **Shy Reward**, The value is real (time saved, status updated, recognition earned) but the UI doesn't surface it clearly. Or it's there but generic ("Success!") with no evidence, consequence, or specificity. **Fix:** make the reward concrete. "Saved 4 minutes" beats "Saved." "Your post is now visible to your 47 followers" beats "Posted."

3. **Over-Reward**, Intensity or frequency wrong for the action. Fullscreen confetti every time the user taps a menu. Animated stars for clicking "next." Cheering for completing a tiny task. **Fix:** match reward magnitude to action significance. Tiny actions get a subtle micro-interaction; major milestones get full celebration.

##### Reward intensity scale

- **Micro-action (tap, toggle):** subtle visual response (color change, small animation, haptic on mobile)
- **Small action (save, edit, complete a field):** quiet confirmation (toast notification, brief checkmark)
- **Meaningful action (post, submit, purchase):** clear success state (success screen, persistent confirmation, summary of what happened)
- **Milestone action (complete onboarding, finish course, hit goal):** real celebration (animation, badge, share prompt, screen takeover)

##### Three rules for designing rewards

1. **Match the reward to the user's emotional state at completion.** Serious moment = clean confirmation. Achievement moment = celebration. Routine moment = quiet acknowledgment.
2. **Make rewards specific, not generic.** "Saved" is a Shy reward. "Saved. Your draft will be auto-published Friday at 9am" is a Control reward done right.
3. **Reward the right thing.** If a user took a hard step, acknowledge the hard part. ("Thanks for canceling, we'll process your refund in 3 business days.") If they took a brave step, acknowledge the bravery. ("You're in. We send one product per day, no fluff.")

#### Common patterns and anti-patterns

##### The "polished but generic" pattern (fails Copywriting)

A beautifully designed hero with "Power your business with our platform" as the headline. No specificity, no benefit, no reason to keep reading. Visual design is doing all the work; copy is doing none. **Fix:** rewrite the headline to name the specific outcome ("Get 10 hours back per week" or "Close deals 3x faster") and make it pass the Copy Swap Test.

##### The "egalitarian hierarchy" pattern (fails Emphasis)

Every button is the same size and same color. Every card looks equally important. The user has no idea where to go next, so they scroll forever or leave. **Fix:** pick the ONE primary action per section. Style it boldly. Style everything else as supporting.

##### The "border bloat" pattern (fails Layout)

Every card has a 1px gray border. Every section has a divider line. Every form field has a box. The page looks "structured" but feels noisy and cluttered. **Fix:** remove most borders. Use whitespace and subtle background shifts to do the same grouping work.

##### The "silent success" pattern (fails Reward)

User submits a form. Page reloads. Form is empty. Did it work? Did it fail? Did anything happen? **Fix:** confirm explicitly with specific language. "Sent. We'll email you within 24 hours" beats a silent reload.

##### The "tracked-out generic CTA" pattern (fails Copywriting AND Emphasis)

A button labeled "GET STARTED" with 0.2em letter-spacing. Looks editorial, communicates nothing. The user knows neither what they're getting nor what's about to happen. **Fix:** specific verb + object, no tracking, and treat the button itself as an emphasis dial (not just the text inside it).

#### When to apply this skill

- **Before writing CSS:** decide what's primary, what's secondary, what each button says
- **When auditing an existing page:** walk through C, L, E, A, R in order
- **When users complain "I don't know what to do here":** apply Emphasis + Copywriting
- **When users complete actions but don't return:** apply Reward
- **When conversion drops on a specific step:** apply Copywriting (button label) + Accessibility (visibility)

#### 1. The mental model (read this once, everything else follows)

The track question: **if an agency were run by agents instead of humans, how would it work?**

A human agency has: a manager who plans and delegates, specialists who execute, handoff notes between them, institutional memory, a boss who can assign work without knowing how it gets done, and a way to know whether the work is getting better. The rubric scores exactly those seven things.

##### The four loops (this is the part you said you wanted to understand)

Every parameter is really measuring one of four loops. If you can spot which loop is broken, you know what to tell the team.

**Loop 1: The delegation loop** (scored by Org structure, 5x)
Manager reads the request → plans subtasks → assigns to specialists → reviews their output → accepts or bounces back for revision. The loop test: give two structurally different requests. If the plan is identical both times, there is no loop, just a fixed pipeline. A bounce-back ("this draft is wrong, redo with X") is the visible proof the manager reviews, not just routes.

**Loop 2: The escalation loop** (scored inside the root parameter and Org L5)
Agent hits an edge case → instead of failing silently or hallucinating, it hands the task to a human WITH full context ("order 4412, missing 2 items, refund eligible per policy, but amount exceeds my limit, approve?"). Escalation by exception is what separates root L4 (human approves everything) from L5 (human touched only when needed). A restart is not an escalation. Context must travel with the handoff.

**Loop 3: The memory loop** (scored by Handoffs and memory, 2x)
Three layers, worth memorizing verbatim because the L5 definition is exactly this:
1. **Now**: the current task (this order, this candidate, this ticket)
2. **This user's past**: previous orders, previous disputes, earlier answers
3. **The business's rules**: refund policy, hiring rubric, escalation limits
The loop test: role-play a user across a handoff. If agent 2 re-asks what agent 1 already knew, the loop is broken at that seam.

**Loop 4: The improvement loop** (scored by Evals, 5x, and enabled by Observability, 7x)
Run fails → failure becomes a test case → prompt/agent gets versioned and changed → the test set is re-run → score demonstrably rises. This is the closed loop at Evals L5. Observability is what makes this loop possible at all: you cannot turn a failure into a test case if you cannot see what happened. That is why observability carries 7x, second only to the root.

**One sentence to give teams:** "Your product is the output (root, 80 pts). Everything else scores how visible, organized, and improvable the machine that produces it is."

#### 3. Level-up playbook, parameter by parameter

The mentor's job in one line per visit: diagnose the current level, name the ONE move to the next level, leave.

##### Working output (root, 20x). The only parameter that can zero the day.
- **L1→L2**: make the agents actually touch a system. One tool call that writes something real, anywhere. "Talking about the task" is L1 no matter how good the conversation looks.
- **L2→L3**: shrink the declared job until success is repeatable. Mock the surfaces: Google Sheet as order DB, Airtable as ATS, sandbox Gmail. Get one usable artifact out reliably (a scorecard, a filed ticket, an updated row). Target 50 to 70% success over 10 runs.
- **L3→L4**: **swap one mocked surface for a real one** and put a human approval gate before anything final moves. This is the single most important move in the track: staged surfaces hard-cap the root at L3, no matter how good the system is. Real = a system a paying customer could use tomorrow: a real Gmail inbox, a real support queue (free Freshdesk on a real store), a real ATS, a real live site, a real repo.
- **L4→L5**: remove the human from the happy path, keep them for exceptions only, and hit 85%+ success across 3+ repeated runs. The path is narrowing scope, not adding capability: cut the task types the crew fails at until what remains is reliable. Escalations must carry full context.
- **At judging**: have 3 to 5 fresh real tasks queued. Each one completed live = +20 overflow.

##### Org structure (5x)
- **L1→L2**: split the monolith prompt into 2 to 3 agents with distinct jobs.
- **L2→L3**: add a named manager that routes to named specialists. Fixed routing table is fine at this level.
- **L3→L4**: the manager must PLAN per request (different requests produce different subtask plans) and REVIEW outputs (at least one visible bounce-back with revision notes in the trace). Tell teams: "your trace needs to show a rejection."
- **L4→L5**: manager spawns a specialist that did not exist at kickoff, stuck agents escalate with the concrete blocker. Honestly out of reach for most teams in 8 hours. Do not push anyone here unless everything else is L4+.
- **Trap to warn about**: agent headcount is not the score. Five agents in a fixed chain is L2. The mentor test is a task that should skip a step: does the pipeline run it anyway?

##### Observability (7x). The cheapest big points on the board.
- **L1→L2**: stop printing, start appending JSONL: one line per agent step (agent, input, output, tokens, timestamp).
- **L2→L3**: any UI where a mentor names a past run and the team opens it and walks it step by step. A simple HTML page over the JSONL file counts. Langfuse free tier counts. Tool-agnostic, the logo does not matter.
- **L3→L4**: trace as a tree (who called whom), token and dollar cost attributed per step, filter by agent or task. The mentor test: "which agent spent the most this morning?" answered from the tool, not memory.
- **L4→L5**: side-by-side diff of two runs, alerts that actually fired, search across runs. Production-grade. Only for teams cruising.
- **Hermes note**: if the team builds on Hermes as harness, its session traces already get them most of L3. Point this out; many teams will not realize they already have the artifact.

##### Evals (5x)
- **L1→L2**: after each change, re-run the same handful of inputs and look. (Do not aim here, pass through it.)
- **L2→L3**: a NAMED set: 15 to 25 inputs with expected outcomes in a file. Run manually before/after each prompt change. Write scores down: "v1 passed 14/20, v2 passed 17/20." That sentence, with the file open, is L3.
- **L3→L4**: script it, run on every change, and a drop actually blocks the change. A shell script that exits nonzero counts as CI-style if it truly gates.
- **L4→L5**: every real failure auto-appends to the eval set, prompts in git, pass rate chart rising across versions. The demo line: "here is Tuesday's failure as test case #23."

##### Handoffs and memory (2x)
- **The one move that matters, L3→L4**: pass a structured context object (user, task state, history so far) with every handoff so the next agent never re-asks. Add a lookup of this user's past interactions.
- **L4→L5**: add the third layer, business rules (refund policy, rubric, limits) applied visibly in decisions. Only 2 more points, so only if time is spare.

##### Cost and latency (1x)
- The lower tier governs: worse of time and cost sets the level. Teams at L2 here usually have a looping agent; fixing the loop is worth more on the root than here. One cheap tip: know your per-run cost from the trace. If a team cannot answer "what does one task cost?", they lose points on two parameters at once.

##### Management UI (1x)
- Max 4 points. Tell every team: do not build this before 15:30. A functional dashboard where a PM could pause an agent and edit a prompt (L3, 2 pts) is the sensible ceiling. L5 is a live test with a volunteer the team does not choose; do not promise it.

#### 4. Questions you will get, with answers

**"Our crew works on Airtable / Google Sheets / sandbox Gmail. Is that okay?"**
Okay for L3, capped there. To score L4+, at least one surface must be real: a system a paying customer could use tomorrow. Cheapest real surfaces in 8 hours: a real Gmail inbox, a real Slack workspace, a real deployed site, a real repo, a free-tier Freshdesk/Zendesk on a real micro-business. The clock-friendly move: build on mocks until 13:00, swap one surface real in the afternoon.

**"How many agents should we have?"**
Wrong question. Two agents with a manager that plans and reviews beats six in a fixed chain. The judge will give a task that should skip a step and watch whether your pipeline runs it anyway.

**"Does the framework matter? CrewAI vs LangGraph vs Hermes subagents?"**
No. Every parameter is tool-agnostic. Judges look at traces and behavior, not logos. Remember eligibility though: Hermes must be either the coding partner (keep session receipts) or the harness your product runs on.

**"If a human approves every step, do we lose points?"**
No, that is exactly root L4 (60 pts). L5 removes the human from the happy path only. Do not delete your approval gate until you are hitting 85%+ across repeated runs; a hallucinated action in front of a judge costs far more than the gate.

**"We do not have money for an observability tool."**
Free is fine, homemade is fine. JSONL log = L2. Simple viewer over it = L3. The question is never "what tool," it is "can the mentor pick yesterday's failed run and watch you walk it."

**"What is an eval set in an 8-hour hackathon?"**
A file. 15 to 25 real inputs with agreed expected outcomes. Run it before and after each prompt change, record the pass count. One hour of work, 10 points.

**"How will they check memory?"**
The mentor role-plays a user across a handoff. If the second agent re-asks anything the first agent knew, you are L3 at best. Pass a context object at every handoff.

**"How do they measure success rate?"**
Live re-runs during judging. L5 needs 85%+ across 3+ repeated runs, and every extra real task completed autonomously in front of the judge pays +20 overflow. Queue real tasks for the demo.

**"Should we build an admin dashboard?"**
It is 1x, max 4 points. Only after the root is L4 and observability is L3+. Most teams that build a pretty dashboard early are burning root-parameter time.

**"Can our agency product also score virality points?"**
Yes: cross-track bonus at half weight, capped at 50. Post the demo clip, collect signups on a landing page. Also stack power-ups: Convex, Linkup, ElevenLabs fit agent crews naturally, +25 each.

**"Our agents complete the task but nothing changes in any system."**
Then nothing was completed. Root L1. Output must LAND: a row written, a ticket updated, an email sent, a commit pushed. This is the first thing to check at every desk.

**"What should we demo?"**
Not a recording, not slides. One fresh real task, triggered live, completed end to end, then open the trace and show the delegation loop. Then run a second task to prove repeatability.
