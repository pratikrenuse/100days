# The Physical World Gets Automated

#thesis
**Awareness:** Building (humanoids are hyped; the supply-chain angle is not)

## Claim
Foundation models become the brain for general-purpose robots, so physical labour gets a software-style cost curve. The durable value sits in the physical supply chain, not in any single robot brand.

## Where it leads (2035 to 2045)
Warehouses, construction, eldercare and manufacturing, sectors that never felt software, get reshaped. Whoever owns the unglamorous inputs (actuators, harmonic drives, tactile sensors, batteries, and the minerals inside them) captures the value, and a lot of that is concentrated in one country.

## Mechanism
Robot hardware is improving, but the real unlock is shared "robot brains" and training data. The scarce inputs are physical components and the refined minerals behind them.

## Hidden players
Figure (Brett Adcock), Tesla Optimus, [[Unitree]] and AgiBot (China volume leaders), [[Agility Robotics]], [[Walden Robotics]], [[EngineAI]], [[LimX Dynamics]], plus the component and materials layer linking to [[Compute and Resources Become Geopolitics]]. Added July 28, 2026: [[Humanoid]] (Europe's wheeled entry, funded by its own customer and factory). Watchlist one layer down: Schaeffler (actuators, ~40% to 60% of a humanoid's bill of materials), Leaderdrive and Zhejiang Shuanghuan Driveline (harmonic and RV reducers). Promoted August 11, 2026 from watchlist to tracked player: [[Nabtesco]] (~60% of cycloidal reducers, operating profit up 60% YoY), with Harmonic Drive Systems (~85% of harmonic drives) as its non-substitutable twin at the precision end.

## Outlook (10 / 20 / 30 year forecast, sourced; projections are bets, not facts)
Added 2026-07-02 to support multi-horizon posts. Full layer-by-layer curve (actuators, hands, brain, power, magnets, labour) with sources and accuracy flags: [[Signals/Humanoid Robots - The Long-Horizon Curve (2026-07-02)]]. Sources: Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, BofA, Citi, Adamas, ICN, TrendForce. Note the forecast dispersion is huge (2050 units range from ~86M to ~1B+), so use ranges, not single points.

**The cost curve (the engine of everything below).** Average humanoid ~$35,000 in 2025, projected to ~$17,000 by 2030 (a ~51% cut in five years), then falling ~20 to 30% per year as volume scales. Tesla targets sub-$20,000 at scale; Hyundai/Boston Dynamics note automotive volume turns a $1,000 robot part into a $100 one. A humanoid behaves like a smartphone with limbs: same learning-curve economics.

**~10 years (to ~2035).**
- Market ~$38 billion (Goldman Sachs, a 6x upgrade on their earlier $6B call). ~250,000+ units shipped per year by 2030; ~1.4 million humanoids in service by 2035.
- Deployment is overwhelmingly industrial: warehouses, factories, logistics. Figure (BMW line, 99% task accuracy, ~40% per-unit cost cut), Tesla Optimus, and China's Unitree/AgiBot scale toward hundreds of thousands of units a year.
- First serious eldercare/medical pilots begin (~2030+), pulled by a WHO-projected ~13 million global nursing shortage by 2030 and shrinking working-age populations (Germany forecasts a >150,000 nurse shortfall by 2030).

**~20 years (to ~2045).**
- A capable humanoid likely costs less than a scooter (sub-$10k on a 20 to 30%/yr decline). Robots spread from factories to anywhere work is repetitive: retail, construction, hospitals, and into homes.
- The aging crunch flips robots from luxury to necessity. As workforces shrink (Japan, Korea, China, Germany), whole economies lean on machines to keep hospitals and factories staffed.
- Morgan Stanley's trajectory implies humanoids generating on the order of $1.6 trillion in annual revenue by 2040.

**~30 years (to ~2055).**
- Morgan Stanley: a ~$5 trillion market by 2050 with ~1 billion humanoids deployed, "as ubiquitous as smartphones." ~930 million in industry/commerce, ~80 million in homes, ~63 million in the US alone.
- Bull case (Musk, Adcock): 1 to 10 billion humanoids worldwide by the 2040s, potentially outnumbering humans. This is where the post-labour questions open: wages, jobs, and the meaning of work once physical labour has a software cost curve.

**The chokepoint (where the durable money sits).** Not the robot brands. The scarce inputs: precision actuators and harmonic-drive gears (Japan's Harmonic Drive Systems dominates the high end), tactile sensors, batteries, and the rare-earth magnets inside every joint ([[MP Materials]], [[Compute and Resources Become Geopolitics]]). Actuators alone are roughly half a humanoid's bill of materials. (Component-supply depth still thin in the brain, worth a dedicated pull.)

## Evidence added September 22, 2026

- **The most valuable robot work this week had no legs, no arms and no anthropomorphic anything.** [[Multiply Labs]] raised a **$75M Series B on September 17, 2026**, past **$100M** total, to scale robotic manufacturing clusters for cell and gene therapies and other advanced biologics. Investors: **AstraZeneca Ventures, Lingotto, Legend Biotech, Strange Ventures**, with **Casdin Capital, Lux Capital and Founders Fund** returning. Company figures, flagged: **74% lower cost per dose**, **up to 100x the throughput** of manual manufacturing, achieved by driving **existing pharmaceutical equipment** rather than replacing it. Cross-listed to [[Biology Becomes a Search Problem]].
- Why this is a correction to how this thesis has been hunting. For six months the note has tracked humanoids and then, correctly, dropped a rung to the components inside them ([[Nabtesco]], Harmonic Drive Systems). Both rungs assume the valuable robot is a **general-purpose body**. Multiply Labs is the counter-example: a fixed robotic cluster doing one high-value task in a clean room, already earning revenue, with an economic case that does not require general manipulation to work at all. The thesis claim survives (physical labour gets a software cost curve) but the sentence "whoever owns the physical supply chain wins" needs a qualifier: **in the near term the money is in the tasks where a human hand is the expensive part, not where a human body is.** A personalised cell therapy is hand-made by technicians at enormous cost per dose. That is a far softer target than a warehouse.
- **New tripwire (added September 22, 2026): special-purpose beats general-purpose to profitability.** If the robot companies that reach durable profit first are fixed-cell, task-specific systems in high-value regulated industries rather than humanoids, then the cost curve this thesis predicts arrives through a completely different door, and the component chokepoint that matters is not reducers and actuators but **regulatory validation** and integration with installed equipment. Watch whether [[Agility Robotics]] (public, AGLT) or any humanoid maker reports positive gross margin on deployed fleets before a special-purpose peer does, and watch whether AstraZeneca or Legend Biotech converts a Multiply Labs pilot into a validated commercial line.
- **Deliberate non-edit, recorded:** no in-window humanoid event surfaced. Aggregate 2026 robotics funding remains hot, but every specific item found (Qualcomm's Dragonwing IQ10 humanoid processor, Figure's valuation, the harmonic-drive supply constraint) is either already in this note or undated in the window. The component rung opened on August 11 with [[Nabtesco]] produced nothing new for a second consecutive run.

## What would break it
- Real-world reliability (mean time between failures) staying poor. Demos are not deployments.
- General-purpose manipulation proving harder/slower than expected.
