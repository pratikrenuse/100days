# Compute and Resources Become Geopolitics

#thesis
**Awareness:** Building to mainstream (rare-earth angle known; the off-world angle is not)

## Claim
The arenas of national power shift to the physical substrate of AI: chips, refined minerals, energy, and increasingly orbit. A country can win the model race and still lose the AI economy.

## Where it leads (2035 to 2045)
The decisive question is not "who built AGI" but "who can physically build and power the machines AGI commands." Control of refining, magnets, and energy matters more than benchmark scores. Compute starts migrating to wherever power and cooling are cheapest, including space.

## Mechanism
If cognition is cheap, leverage moves to the scarce physical inputs. One country dominates the refining of those inputs and has begun using that as leverage. Meanwhile cost-to-orbit is collapsing, opening an entirely new place to put compute.

## Hidden players
[[Starcloud]] and Google Project Suncatcher (orbital compute), [[MP Materials]] and Lynas (ex-China rare earths), magnet and battery-material reshoring names. Added September 15, 2026: [[Stoke Space]] (the second credible fully reusable launch vehicle, which is what every orbital-compute plan actually depends on) and [[Suniva]] cross-listed from [[Energy is the Ceiling]] (the solar cell rung the US stopped making). Added September 22, 2026: [[Impulse Space]] (movement **between** orbits, the step after getting up there, which every orbital-compute plan assumes and none of them has costed).

## Evidence for (dated)
- China controls ~90% of rare-earth processing, 80% tungsten, 60% antimony, 80%+ of the battery supply chain.
- 2026 export controls pushed some rare-earth prices up ~6x; EU export-license approvals fell below 25%.
- Western answer "FORGE" (54-nation alliance, Feb 2026); but a new separation plant takes 5 to 7 years (IEA).
- First AI model trained in orbit (Dec 2025, Starcloud, Nvidia-backed); solar in dawn-dusk orbit up to 8x more productive; vacuum as a free heat sink.
- The leverage is now being used by name: China's Ministry of Commerce added 10 US firms, including [[MP Materials]] and USA Rare Earth, to its export-control list. (June 22, 2026) Entity-specific targeting, a step beyond blanket quotas, and a backhanded confirmation that the West's one scaled magnet pure-play is strategic.
- No terbium or dysprosium oxide shipments from China to Japan recorded since November 2025, showing how tight the heavy rare-earth squeeze has become. (2026)
- The controls grew an enforcement arm: effective July 1, 2026, China stood up a whistleblower mechanism inviting reports of suspected violations of strategic-mineral dual-use export controls, alongside customs-smuggling enforcement in a rare-earth matter. Rule-writing has turned into policing, which makes the leverage durable. (July 2026, Morgan Lewis client alert)
- Orbit became a filing land-grab: Orbital, a five-month-old, six-person LA startup with $5M pre-seed, filed an FCC application for up to 100,000 data-center satellites totaling 10 GW of compute (2-ton satellites, 100 kW arrays, sun-synchronous orbit, demo next year, Orbital-1 in 2028). It follows SpaceX's January 2026 filing for up to 1M satellites (100 GW). The scarce resource being claimed is orbital slots and spectrum, on paper, years before hardware. (filed June 24, reported July 8 to 9, 2026, SpaceNews / DCD)
- The West's rare-earth answer is fighting itself: MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, both on China's June 22 entity list, are locked in a trade-secrets lawsuit over magnet technology (USA Rare Earth denied all claims in a Texas Business Court filing). The two companies meant to break the chokehold are suing each other. (July 2, 2026, The National / Mining.com)
- The truce did not restore the flow: US-bound Chinese rare-earth magnet shipments in the first half of 2026 ran about 20% below the 2022 to 2024 average, despite last year's trade truce. Magnets containing controlled heavy rare earths, chiefly dysprosium and terbium, remain licence-gated, and the early-2026 catalogue expansion captures any foreign-made product containing 0.1% or more Chinese-origin rare earths, or made with Chinese processing technology. The constraint is structural, not tactical. (Bloomberg, July 20, 2026; CSIS; IEA commentary)
- Sovereign compute is now being sold in gigawatts by the chip vendor: Nvidia and KAIST launched a joint AI research lab in Seoul on July 23 to 24, 2026, a $300M collaboration including roughly $50M per year of compute over an initial five years. Three days later, NAVER, Nvidia and Brookfield announced an expansion of Korea's national AI factory infrastructure from the initial 55 MW Nvidia DSX deployment to 200 MW, with NAVER stating an intent to reach 1 GW. (Nvidia newsroom / GlobeNewswire, July 23 and 27, 2026)
- Compute financing became a national-scale credit question: Nvidia is reported in talks to guarantee roughly $250B so OpenAI can lease SB Energy's planned 10 GW Piketon campus, with a parallel talk on funding chip purchases reported as high as $350B. When one vendor's balance sheet underwrites both the chips and the buildings, the concentration is strategic, not just financial. (July 27, 2026, WSJ via Qz / Al Jazeera. Reported, not signed. See [[SB Energy]])
- Orbit moved from filing to hardware, with named silicon in it. **SpaceX and Nvidia announced on August 4, 2026** that Nvidia will design the compute payload for **Starmind AI1**, the first satellite in a planned orbital network built to run production AI workloads, using Nvidia Vera CPUs, Rubin GPUs and the Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale system. Read against the June filings (SpaceX for up to 1M satellites, Blue Origin for 51,600, [[Orbital]] for 100,000), the sequence is now paper claim, then payload, and the same vendor supplies the chips on the ground and above it. (SpaceNews / Interesting Engineering, August 4 to 6, 2026)
- The memory that AI runs on stopped being allocated by price and started being allocated by queue. Q2 2026 DRAM contract prices rose 58% to 63% quarter over quarter and NAND 70% to 75%; Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are reported effectively sold out of 2027 DRAM and HBM output, with buyers placing advance deposits ahead of formal long-term agreements. AI servers and HBM take close to 70% of DRAM output, squeezing phone and PC makers' 2027 allocations. When a strategic input is rationed by deposit rather than by market, allocation becomes a policy question. (Counterpoint via Sammy Fans, August 4, 2026; Tom's Hardware)
- **A new controlled material entered the chain, and it is the one the lasers are made of.** Indium phosphide has been on China's export control list since February 4, 2025, so every overseas shipment by [[AXT]], the substrate supplier, needs a permit from China's Ministry of Commerce. AXT's InP revenue tracks the permit count rather than demand: $3.6M a year ago, $13.6M the prior quarter, a record $30.7M in Q2/2026, with roughly $66M of Q3 revenue either already permitted or not requiring one. In the same window TrendForce (August 6, 2026) reported the InP shortage as the AI optical interconnect bottleneck, with Lumentum's CEO putting the gap above DRAM and NAND. Because indium is a zinc smelting by-product, this is a chokepoint that a licensing decision can tighten and capital cannot loosen. (Semiconductor Today, August 4, 2026; TrendForce, August 6, 2026)
- **Rare earths, status check.** The April 2025 regime licensing dysprosium, terbium and the magnets containing them was never suspended and remains fully in force in 2026. The statutory licensing target is 45 working days, about nine weeks, but heavy rare-earth approvals in practice run 60 to 120 days or more, and the extraterritorial provisions carry a November 2026 enforcement date. New non-China magnet capacity begins coming online in summer 2026, which is the first real test of the "ex-China refining actually commissions" tripwire below. (CSIS / IEA / trade compliance reporting, reviewed August 11, 2026. See [[MP Materials]])
- **Industrial policy is now funding the layer under the fab.** South Korea announced a 5 trillion won fund, about $3.5B, for semiconductor materials, equipment, components and fabless design, plus another 5 trillion won of trade financing for export-oriented suppliers, inside a strategy to unlock more than $576B of semiconductor manufacturing investment with accelerated permits for chip clusters and dedicated work on the electricity, water, transport and land underneath them. Separately, Naver is working with Nvidia and Brookfield on a Korean AI data-centre expansion that could reach gigawatt scale, with Brookfield providing up to $9B of financing and Nvidia planning a $1B strategic investment subject to conditions. Sovereign compute has stopped meaning "buy GPUs" and started meaning "own the supply chain and the power." (Reuters / WSJ, reported August 10, 2026)
- **China's private launch sector keeps closing the reuse gap.** LandSpace targeted August 10, 2026 for the second flight of Zhuque-3, its 66-metre stainless-steel methane and liquid-oxygen rocket, including a second attempt to recover the first stage. The maiden flight reached orbit in December 2025 but lost the booster on descent. Reusable methane launch is the cost mechanism behind both mega-constellations and any orbital-compute plan, so this is the same contest as [[Orbital]] and Starmind, run from the other side. (Space.com via TechStartups, August 10, 2026)
- **The Falcon 9 replacement got a billion dollars, and it is not from SpaceX.** [[Stoke Space]] raised a **$1B Series E at a $10B valuation** on September 8, 2026, an initial closing co-led by Point72 Ventures and Spark Capital with General Innovation, Glade Brook, US Innovation Technology, Washington Harbour Partners, Woven Capital and Y Combinator, taking total capital raised to roughly $2.3B. Two vehicles: **Nova Pathfinder**, three metric tons to low Earth orbit, first orbital launch targeted **early 2027**; and **Nova Block 2**, 15 metric tons to LEO in a 5-metre fairing, first launch targeted **2029**. Both stages fully reusable. Why this thesis and not a general space note: every orbital-compute plan in this brain, [[Starcloud]], [[Orbital]], SpaceX's Starmind and Blue Origin's 51,600-satellite filing, rests on one assumption, that cost to orbit collapses. So far that assumption has had exactly one supplier. A second fully reusable vehicle with a billion dollars behind it and a named 2027 date is the first serious evidence that the orbital leg of this thesis does not depend on a single company's roadmap. Honest caveat: Stoke has not yet flown to orbit, and "early 2027" is a target, not a launch. (Stoke Space / Space.com / GeekWire, September 8, 2026. See [[Stoke Space]])
- **Getting to orbit was only the first half, and the second half just got funded.** [[Impulse Space]] announced a **$308M Series D extension on September 16, 2026**, bringing the combined Series D to **$808M**, from 137 Ventures, BANNER VC, DFJ Growth, Linse Capital, Lux Capital and Valor Equity Partners. The company was founded by **Tom Mueller, SpaceX's first employee**, who led development of the Merlin and Raptor engines. It builds in-space transportation: **Mira**, a precision manoeuvring orbital transfer vehicle already flying, and **Helios**, a high-energy kick stage with first flight scheduled for **2027**, plus a rideshare programme, Caravan. In the same period it took a **$28M contract extension from the US Space Force** for the VICTUS SALO 2 and 3 responsive-space missions and was selected into the **NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1 provider pool** with Helios, the first non-traditional upper-stage developer to act as a prime contractor under that programme. The June 2026 Series D was $500M at $4.26B; the extension valuation is reported by some outlets at $5.4B and is flagged as **reported, not company-confirmed**. Why it belongs here: [[Stoke Space]] answered whether there is a second way up. Nobody in this brain had asked what happens **after**. Every orbital-compute filing, [[Starcloud]], [[Orbital]], SpaceX's Starmind, Blue Origin's 51,600 satellites, silently assumes that thousands of heavy satellites reach a specific orbit, hold it against drag, get repositioned and eventually get disposed of. That is a different vehicle, a different propulsion problem and, as of this week, a different company with $808M. Launch is the ticket; this is the taxi. (GlobeNewswire / Payload / SatNews, September 16, 2026. See [[Impulse Space]])
- **China's flagship accelerator is a sovereignty product, and it still has an American dependency in it.** Alibaba unveiled the **Zhenwu V900** at the Apsara Conference on **September 21 to 22, 2026**: three times the performance of its predecessor, **216 GB of HBM**, **1.2 TB/s inter-chip bandwidth**, FP8 and FP4 precision, scaling to **500,000 chips per cluster**, mass production and commercial release targeted **Q1 2027**. Alongside it, a commitment to **more than 20 GW of global data-centre capacity by 2032** and a planned **5-to-10-trillion-parameter** model. Shares rose about 3% in Hong Kong. Coverage frames the chip explicitly as a response to Nvidia's absence from the Chinese market under export controls. The geopolitical reading: export controls are producing exactly the domestic substitute they were designed to delay, on a published timeline, at cluster scale. The counter-reading, equally important: 216 GB of HBM per chip means the substitute does not escape the memory chokepoint, it inherits it. (CNBC / Reuters / Benzinga, September 21 to 22, 2026)
- **Rare earths, status check for this window: nothing new and dated.** The substantive escalations remain 2025 and mid-2026 (China's October 2025 FDPR-style package, suspended for one year to November 10, 2026; the widening to 10 US firms in June and 14 EU firms in July 2026). **Xi's September 24 Washington visit had not happened as of this run** and remains the next hard date for this thesis. Logged so the absence is a recorded check rather than a gap.
- Scale of the physical buildout, from Exponential View's "State of the AI Economy" (June 25, 2026): ~$2 trillion cumulative hyperscaler+neocloud CapEx through 2026 (+$535bn above the pre-AI trend), increasingly debt-financed via neoclouds (risk moving outside the firms); Nvidia supply commitments grew $31bn to $95bn in a year; global compute is on a ~66% CAGR since 1971, lifting toward ~80% with AI. The report's scope is explicitly "global ex-China," and the open-weight commoditization pressure it documents comes largely from Chinese labs (DeepSeek, MiniMax, Moonshot/Kimi, ByteDance's Doubao), the model-race front of this same geopolitics. See [[Signals/EV State of the AI Economy (2026-06-25)]].

## What would break it
- A rare-earth substitution breakthrough (magnets without the scarce elements).
- Orbital compute staying a stunt rather than scaling.
- Tripwires: ex-China refining capacity actually commissioning; Starship reaching reliable high-cadence reuse.
- **Sharpened September 15, 2026: the launch-monopoly assumption is no longer load-bearing on one company.** Every orbital claim in this thesis has implicitly been a bet on SpaceX's cost curve. [[Stoke Space]]'s $1B at $10B, with a fully reusable vehicle targeting orbit in early 2027 and a 15-tonne follow-on in 2029, means the cost-to-orbit variable now has at least two credible suppliers. That strengthens the orbital leg of the thesis and weakens the "orbital compute is a stunt" breaker above, but only conditionally: it converts on the first Nova flight, not on the round. Watch the early 2027 Nova Pathfinder date, and watch whether any orbital-compute filer names a launch provider other than SpaceX.
- **New tripwire (added September 22, 2026): the orbital bill has a second line nobody has costed.** Every orbital-compute number in this thesis is a launch-cost number. [[Impulse Space]]'s $808M says the industry now prices a separate problem: getting a satellite to the **right** orbit, keeping it there, moving it, and taking it down. For a constellation of 100,000 two-tonne satellites, station-keeping and disposal are not a rounding error on the launch bill, they are a recurring propellant cost for the life of the fleet. If any orbital data-centre filer publishes a **total** cost per delivered kilowatt-year in orbit rather than a cost per kilogram to orbit, that is the moment the economics get tested honestly. Watch the Helios first flight in 2027, and watch whether [[Starcloud]] or [[Orbital]] names an in-space mobility provider in a filing.
- **Sharpened September 22, 2026: export controls are now producing measurable substitution on a published schedule.** The Zhenwu V900's Q1 2027 production target, at 500,000 chips per cluster, converts the standing question "do controls delay China or does China route around them" from argument into a date. The honest version of this thesis's claim gets narrower and stronger as a result: controls do not stop a determined state from building accelerators, they force it to build them **around a memory chokepoint it does not own**. Watch whether the V900's HBM is domestically sourced by the Q1 2027 ramp, because that single fact decides whether the control regime has any remaining teeth.
- **New tripwire (added September 15, 2026): the domestic rung gets bought back.** [[Suniva]]'s $835M for 4.5 GW of US solar cell capacity (September 9, 2026) is the same shape as the rare-earth reshoring story one industry over: a country that assembles the finished product discovering it does not make the part in the middle. If this pattern repeats, a US or allied buyer taking the missing middle rung in a second supply chain, then "reshore the chokepoint" stops being rare-earth-specific policy and becomes the default industrial response. Watch for the next one, most likely in magnets, anodes or optical substrates ([[AXT]]).

## Outlook (10/20/30 curve, sourced)
Full deep-research curve (rare earths, orbital compute, chip geopolitics, with bear case): [[Signals/Compute & Resources Geopolitics - Long-Horizon Curve (2026-07-02)]]. Headline: China holds ~91% of REE refining and ~94% of magnet manufacturing (the chokepoint is the magnet, not the mine), and weaponized it via 2025 export controls; a new ex-China chain takes 5-10 years. Taiwan makes ~92% of advanced logic (a war modeled at ~$10.6T year one); US advanced-logic share goes 0% -> 28% by 2032 (SIA/BCG), diversification not de-risking, with ASML/EUV the deepest chokepoint. Orbital compute is real but early (first AI trained in orbit Dec 2025); it pencils out only if launch cost hits ~$200/kg (Google's mid-2030s projection) and cooling, not radiation, is the wall.
