# Post Format (LinkedIn)

Grounded in [[LinkedIn Creator Playbook]], our study of how the best tech and business creators actually write. The job of a post: teach a curious, smart reader one concrete thing they did not know, then land a strong insight that shifts how they see it. They should finish feeling smarter and want to repeat it.

## The core writing principle (outranks everything except the hard rules)
Every sentence has one job: to make them read the next one. That is the whole game. Keep sentences short. Drip the information, never dump it. End a line on a small open question or a slight cliffhanger, then pay it off on the next line and open another ("Here's the catch:", "Sounds insane, I know. But...", "Why now? One reason:"). After every line, ask: does this make me want the next line? If not, cut it or rewrite it.

This matters most in the opening. The instant you answer the hook ("it's a gas turbine"), the curiosity gap closes and the reader can leave. So open a bigger loop immediately ("Yeah, a gas turbine. The same boring machine may decide how fast AI can grow. I had to know why."). Never let the first reveal be a dead end.

## Answer these five before writing (do not skip)
1. Who is this for? (a smart, curious person who wants to feel ahead on tech and the future)
2. What do they currently believe that is incomplete? (for example, "AI is all about the chips")
3. What is the key insight that shifts that belief? (this is the takeaway)
4. What concrete, dated facts prove it? (this is what makes the takeaway earned, not hollow)
5. What should they feel at the end? (smarter, a little ahead of the crowd, ready to share)

## One single takeaway
Before writing, decide the ONE brag-worthy thing the reader should walk away with (for GE Vernova: "AI's real bottleneck is electricity, not chips"). Write it in the file header so we are clear internally. Do NOT write it as a labeled line in the post. Everything in the piece, including the outlook, ladders up to that one idea. We can interlink many facts, but they must all serve the single takeaway, not introduce three competing ones.

## The structure
1. **Hook.** One or two lines, under ~10 words where you can. A curiosity gap or a contrarian reframe. Specific and surprising. Hint, do not spoil. It has to survive the "see more" fold alone.
2. **Rehook.** One short line that raises the stakes and pulls them in ("Here is the part almost nobody talks about").
3. **Body.** The concrete, dated facts as a clear cause-and-effect story. Teach one mechanism. Short paragraphs, heavy white space, simple words. Include one clean bullet list of the hardest facts. Cut jargon a normal reader cannot parse (gigawatt backlogs, basis points, raw specs). Translate a stat into plain language ("as much electricity as all of Japan") or drop it. Lean the weight toward simple, future-facing points over complex present-day metrics.
4. **The shift.** State the key insight plainly. It must follow from the facts above, not float free.
5. **The 10 and 20 year outlook.** Make it a concrete, dated forecast, not vague hedging. Walk a short timeline with named milestones from our notes: what is locked in now (for example, turbines booked through ~2029), what changes around 2030 (first small modular reactors switch on, wind and solar reach about half of new demand, fusion's first grid attempts), and the bolder 2040s bets (fusion at scale, Starcloud and compute leaving Earth). Anchor big numbers with a relatable comparison ("about as much electricity as all of Japan") rather than a pile of raw figures. Then close the forecast with one clear directional call that ladders to the single takeaway. Avoid mushy words like "probably" and "the likely path"; say what happens and when, and mark genuine bets as bets ("has promised", "if launch costs keep falling"). This dated interlink across the brain is the edge no single article has. Keep it readable, not a stat dump.
6. **Close.** One line that lands the throughline (today / in ten years / in twenty), then a simple, easy-to-answer question.

## Make it sound human
Good content still has to read like a person, not a brief. Use contractions (it's, can't, here's). Vary sentence length, let some lines be short and plain. Cut anything that sounds like a press release. Read it aloud once: if you would not say it to a smart friend, rewrite it.

Talk straight to the reader. Use "you" and "I" throughout, so it feels like I am telling you what I found, not a report narrating from nowhere ("I went down a rabbit hole on this", "you basically can't buy one", "here's where I land"). A few first-person reactions ("the part that got me", "it surprised me") do more for warmth than any clever line.

Anchor the unfamiliar to the familiar. When the hidden player is unknown (Starcloud), connect it to a name everyone knows (SpaceX, Bezos, Musk) so the future feels real, not abstract. And interlink the crucial connections the brain already holds: if the story is compute in space, SpaceX's Starship and the collapse in launch cost belong in it. A recognizable name is the handle the reader grabs onto.

Do not pile up obscure company names like an analyst brief. That is the fastest way to sound like AI. Keep the household names (SpaceX, Bezos), and turn the rest into plain phrases: "a few startups are chasing fusion" beats "Helion, Commonwealth Fusion, Oklo, and X-energy." Same for stats: deliver them the way you would say them out loud, not the way a report prints them ("almost 100 times cheaper", not "a roughly 99% reduction in cost per kilogram").

## Length and formatting
- Target ~300 to 400 words, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 characters. Always comfortably under LinkedIn's 3,000 character cap, so check the count. Shorter and simpler beats long and dense.
- Short paragraphs, lots of white space. No walls of text.
- One scannable bullet list of dated facts (3 to 4 lines, not more).

## What "strong takeaway" means (we kept failing here)
- It is a claim that reframes the facts, like "the AI race is really a race for electricity." It is specific and true given everything above it.
- It is NOT a metaphor standing in for an idea ("the toll booth is going up"), NOT a vague mood ("funny where the money hides"), and NOT a grand unprovable prophecy. If you cannot point to the facts in the post that force the conclusion, it is not earned. Cut it and find the real one.

## Hard rules
- No em dashes. No emojis.
- Every number is dated and lives in the relevant Player or Thesis note, sourced.
- The hook is one specific hidden player or unseen cause, never a broad theme.
- One hidden player is never used as a lead twice.
- Write every transition fresh. Never reuse a stock opener or borrowed scaffolding.

## The test
- Would a smart person screenshot this and send it to a friend, saying "did you know this?"
- Can you say the takeaway in one sentence, and is it proven by a fact in the post?
- If you swapped the company name out, would it still read as generic? If yes, it is not specific enough.
