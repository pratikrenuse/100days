# SK Hynix - The Memory Wall

**Hidden player:** [[SK Hynix]]
**Thesis:** [[The Bottleneck Slides Down the Stack]]
**Status:** Final (locked voice + format)
**Date:** June 2026

---

I went down a rabbit hole this week trying to work out who actually makes the money in AI, and I came out fixated on a company I had genuinely never heard of.

SK Hynix. Korean. Makes memory chips.

..

Everyone knows Nvidia. Its chips train basically every big AI model on earth.

What I didn't know is that an Nvidia AI chip isn't really one chip. It's a little package.

The processor sits in the middle, and pressed right up against it are stacks of memory called HBM, high bandwidth memory.

Without them the processor just sits there starving. It can do the math, it just has nothing to do the math on.

And the memory, it turns out, is the hard part. Not the processor.

..

Here is why. HBM isn't one chip lying flat. It's eight or twelve memory chips piled on top of each other, wired straight down through the silicon, jammed millimeters from the processor so the data barely has to travel.

It is miserable to manufacture. The yields are so bad that only three companies on the planet can do it at all: SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron.

And the one Nvidia leans on most is SK Hynix. They make about half the world's supply, and they make the good stuff.

..

This is where it gets a little mad.

- Their HBM is sold out, booked through next year with reservations piling up beyond
- AI data centers now eat something like 70% of all the memory the world makes
- Their own chairman reckons the crunch could last until 2030

A company most people couldn't pick out of a lineup quietly became one of the two or three firms that decide how fast AI is allowed to grow. SK Hynix has a bad year, everyone above them has a bad year.

..

And that's the bit I can't stop turning over.

Ten years ago, the company that would come to define this whole era was making graphics cards for gamers. Nobody was watching Nvidia then either.

The real winner usually sits one rung below the thing everyone's shouting about, in some corner of the supply chain that looks boring right until it's the only thing that matters.

Today it's a memory firm in Korea. In a few years it'll be another name you and I haven't heard yet.

The trick was never picking which AI is smartest. It's finding the dull, critical company the whole thing quietly runs through, before everyone else clocks it. Right now, almost nobody has.
