### Who they are
Founder and CEO of Figure (Figure AI), the Sunnyvale humanoid robotics company valued around $39B as of early 2026. Previously founded Archer Aviation (eVTOL air taxis) and Vettery (talent marketplace, exited). Now building general purpose humanoid robots for both the factory and the home.

### Their future thesis (one line)
General purpose humanoids will join the labor force at scale, drive the cost of physical labor toward the cost of renting a robot, and eventually put a working robot in every home.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Figure Master Plan: Roadmap to a positive future powered by AI" (May 20, 2022), https://www.figure.ai/master-plan
  Adcock's foundational manifesto. He frames humanoids as the answer to a permanent labor shortage, arguing manual labor is roughly 50% of global GDP (about $42T per year) and that robots will push that cost down until it equals the price of renting a machine. He names three long term markets: physical labor, the consumer household (2.3B households plus an aging population needing care), and off-world work to build new worlds on other planets. The plan is explicitly a decades long, 30-year-view bet.

- "Figure CEO predicts big for humanoids, eVTOLs, and memory AI in 2026" (Jan 2026), https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/science-fiction-become-reality-in-2026
  Coverage of Adcock's annual LinkedIn prediction thread. His headline claim: in 2026 humanoids will start working autonomously and performing unsupervised, multi-day tasks in homes they have never seen before. He ties this to a broader shift toward multimodal memory agents in AI. The benchmark he repeats is a robot doing seven to ten hours of useful home work per day with zero human intervention.

- "Forbes: Figure Plans to Ship 100,000 Humanoid Robots Over Next 4 Years" (Jan 30, 2025), https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2025/01/30/figure-plans-to-ship-100000-humanoid-robots-over-next-4-years/
  Lays out the production ramp thesis: scaling from thousands of units to a target of 1 million robots per year, manufactured at the company's BotQ high-volume facility. This is the concrete bridge between the manifesto and reality, where unit cost and volume manufacturing become the gating constraints rather than the science.

- "TIME: The Robot in Your Kitchen / Figure 03 reveal" (Oct 2025), https://time.com/7324233/figure-03-robot-humanoid-reveal/
  Tied to the Figure 03 launch, the robot designed for the home. Adcock argues that within roughly 10 years every home will have a humanoid. The piece marks the public pivot from industrial pilots toward consumer households as the larger long term market.

### Hooks for the brain
- The "cost of labor collapses to the cost of renting a robot" framing is a clean, quotable economic thesis. Pairs well with content on what marketers and small businesses do when physical labor stops being a budget line.
- Adcock controls three of the most cited near-future verticals at once: humanoids (Figure), air taxis (Archer), and gun detection (Cover). A "one founder, three futures" angle.
- The recurring concrete benchmark (7 to 10 hours of unsupervised home work per day) is a great yardstick to track yearly. Good for a "are we there yet" running scorecard.
- Hidden players to watch alongside him: BotQ as a manufacturing story, Helix as Figure's vision-language-action model (the brain, not the body). The software, not the hardware, may be the real moat.

- Paywall: no (X itself is free to read; some interviews are platform-gated)

### Who they are
CEO of Tesla and SpaceX, founder and head of xAI, owner of X. The single most influential individual across rockets, humanoid robots (Tesla Optimus), and frontier AI, and the loudest voice on multiplanetary civilization.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Humanity must become multiplanetary to secure the future of consciousness, and Tesla's AI and robotics (Optimus, FSD, xAI) are the economic engine on Earth that funds getting there.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "SpaceX shifts focus from Mars to a self-growing city on the Moon" (Feb 2026), https://time.com/7373155/elon-musk-mars-moon-city/
  A major 2026 reversal. Musk announced SpaceX would prioritize building a self-sustaining city on the Moon, achievable in under 10 years in his framing, and pushed the realistic horizon for humans on Mars out by roughly five to seven years (now mid-to-late 2030s). His stated logic: the Moon is reachable every 10 days with a 2-day trip versus Mars every 26 months with a 6-month trip, so the Moon is the faster path to securing civilization. This deprioritized the previously planned uncrewed Starship Mars flights in the late-2026 window.

- "SpaceX Mars plan: Starships carrying Optimus robots" (2025, pre-pivot roadmap), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Mars_colonization_program
  The roadmap that defined his stated ambition before the 2026 Moon pivot: uncrewed Starships landing on Mars carrying Tesla Optimus robots to set up power, infrastructure, and scout for water ice before any humans arrive. Crewed landings were floated for as early as 2029 with a self-sustaining colony by roughly 2050. Even with the timeline pushed back, this Optimus-first colonization concept remains his core long-term vision and a useful reference point.

- "Tesla repurposes Fremont lines to build Optimus humanoid robots" (Feb 2026), https://www.teslarati.com/elon-musk-spacex-moon-city-before-mars/
  Signals how load-bearing Optimus has become to the whole thesis. Tesla is ending Model S and Model X production and repurposing those Fremont lines for Optimus. In Musk's framing Optimus, FSD, Dojo, and xAI are not parallel bets, they are the capital and technology engine on Earth that makes an interplanetary civilization financially viable.

- "Elon Musk says Mars in 2026 is 50/50" (mid-2025), https://www.space.com/space-exploration/private-spaceflight/elon-musk-says-spacex-will-launch-its-biggest-starship-yet-this-year-but-mars-in-2026-is-50-50
  A characteristic Musk timeline statement showing how he sets aggressive public targets and then revises them. He gave the 2026 uncrewed Mars window a 50/50 chance before later pivoting to the Moon. Useful as a calibration note: his stated dates are direction and ambition, not commitments, which is exactly why pairing him with a skeptic like Rodney Brooks is valuable.

### Hooks for the brain
- The 2026 Moon-before-Mars pivot is a clean case study in how even the boldest roadmaps slip. Pairs perfectly with Rodney Brooks on "different speeds" and with a "scoring the predictions" running feature.
- The "Optimus funds Mars" framing ties humanoid robots and space into one economic story. Strong spine for a piece on how Earth-side robotics pays for off-world ambition.
- Musk is the hub node: he connects to Adcock (humanoid rival), Beck (launch rival), Berger (the journalist who tracks and fact-checks him), and the AI thread via xAI. He is the natural center of the robotics-and-space map.
- Caution flag for the brain: Musk's claims need a fact-check layer. Always cross-reference his dates against Eric Berger's reporting before treating any timeline as real.
- Tesla ending Model S and Model X to build robots is a vivid, concrete "the future arrived on the factory floor" detail worth a standalone post.

## 80. Peter Beck

#source
Domain: Robotics and space
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.rocketlabusa.com/ (now branded Rocket Lab; corporate site)
- Blog: no personal blog. Rocket Lab updates and mission pages at https://www.rocketlabusa.com/missions/ and the company newsroom
- RSS: unverified (no personal feed; Rocket Lab investor and press releases are the closest thing)
- X: https://x.com/Peter_J_Beck (handle @Peter_J_Beck) and company https://x.com/RocketLab
- Podcast / other: frequent long-form interviews (Space.com Q&As, PBS NewsHour, satellite industry press), Rocket Lab earnings calls, conference keynotes. Knighted, so sometimes "Sir Peter Beck"
- Paywall: no

### Who they are
Founder and CEO of Rocket Lab, the New Zealand-born launch and space-systems company behind the Electron rocket and the in-development reusable Neutron rocket. Self-taught engineer turned billionaire building what he calls an end-to-end space company.

### Their future thesis (one line)
The future belongs to vertically integrated end-to-end space companies that build their own rockets, satellites, and components and operate them in orbit, with Neutron unlocking the launch capacity the industry will lack from 2026 to 2030.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Rocket Lab CEO Peter Beck says Neutron is on track for Q4 2026" (2026), https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/markets/equity/rocket-lab-ceo-peter-beck-says-neutron-is-on-track-for-q4-2026/cZJvqTiRIDx
  Status check on the reusable medium-lift Neutron rocket, his bid to compete with SpaceX Falcon 9. He frames a real launch-capacity constraint in the 2026 to 2030 window that the industry must solve, and positions Neutron as the right vehicle. Notably he calls Neutron mission-enabling but not mission-critical, signaling that the company is more than its rockets. The Archimedes engine is in the hard, "nasty" test phase that gates the timeline.

- "If it's possible, it must be done: Beck eyes missions to Mars and Venus" (2024 to 2026 ongoing), https://www.space.com/space-exploration/if-its-possible-it-must-be-done-rocket-lab-ceo-peter-beck-has-his-eyes-on-missions-to-mars-and-venus
  Lays out his deep-space ambitions. Rocket Lab built the spacecraft for NASA's ESCAPADE Mars mission and is planning a privately funded mission to search for life in the clouds of Venus, which Beck calls a passion project. His reasoning: the Venusian cloud layer is one of the best places in the solar system to look for life, and if it is possible, it must be done. Neutron is meant to be the backbone that extends this interplanetary reach.

- "Peter Beck's vision of Rocket Lab extends beyond himself / end-to-end space company" (interviews 2023 to 2025), https://www.space.com/rocket-lab-peter-beck-interview-april-2024
  The clearest statement of his core strategy. He wants Rocket Lab to build a satellite from its own components, launch it on its own rocket, and operate it in orbit, becoming a true end-to-end space player rather than just a launch provider. He predicts the large successful space companies of the future will copy this vertically integrated model. He frames the goal as a long-term, multi-generational, sustainable space business.

- "Inside Rocket Lab's effort to outpace larger space rivals" (PBS NewsHour, recent), https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/inside-rocket-labs-effort-to-outpace-larger-space-rivals
  A useful outside lens on his strategy of competing with much larger rivals by moving fast and integrating vertically. Good context for how a second-mover challenger positions against SpaceX. Reinforces that the company's edge is the full stack (launch plus satellites plus components plus operations), not any single rocket.

### Hooks for the brain
- Beck is the credible number-two challenger to SpaceX. Any "who else is shaping space" map needs him as the counterweight to Musk, and the Neutron vs Falcon 9 race is concrete and dated.
- The "end-to-end space company" thesis (own the rocket, the satellite, the components, the operations) is a clean strategic frame that generalizes beyond space. Good analogy fuel for vertical-integration arguments.
- The privately funded Venus life-search mission is a striking, quotable story ("if it's possible, it must be done"). Strong narrative hook for a piece on private actors chasing big scientific questions states will not fund.
- Watch the launch-capacity squeeze he flags for 2026 to 2030. If he is right, launch becomes the bottleneck for the whole space economy, which is a trackable, falsifiable prediction.
- His self-taught, no-degree path to running a public rocket company is a builder-archetype story that fits a maker audience.

## 81. Rodney Brooks

#source
Domain: Robotics and space
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://rodneybrooks.com/
- Blog: https://rodneybrooks.com/blog/
- RSS: https://rodneybrooks.com/feed/ (verified, working WordPress feed)
- X: unverified (Brooks is not a heavy X poster; his blog is the primary channel. Robust.ai sometimes mirrors his predictions at https://www.robust.ai/blog/)
- Podcast / other: Robust.ai (his current company) https://www.robust.ai/, plus his archived MIT and iRobot history
- Paywall: no

### Who they are
MIT robotics pioneer (former director of the MIT Computer Science and AI Lab), co-founder of iRobot (Roomba) and Rethink Robotics, now co-founder and CTO of Robust.ai. He is the field's most credentialed skeptic, in AI research since 1976.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Almost everything in robotics and AI is hyped on a misread of timelines, and humanoid romanticism in particular will disappoint because dexterity and safe bipedal walking are far harder than demos suggest.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Predictions Scorecard, 2026 January 01" (Jan 1, 2026), https://rodneybrooks.com/predictions-scorecard-2026-january-01/
  Brooks's eighth annual self-graded scorecard on dated predictions he made in 2018, which he has committed to scoring every January 1 until 2050. His verdict on himself: held up well, but he was if anything a little too optimistic. Central methodological point: people confuse the very different speeds of research, hype, large-scale deployment, and economic change. He introduced a "hemming and hawing" category for tech that looks deployed but secretly has humans in the loop, so the public believes a capability has arrived when it has not.

- "Five long-term predictions for 2026 to 2036" (made March 26, 2025, tracked in the 2026 scorecard above), https://rodneybrooks.com/predictions-scorecard-2026-january-01/
  His undated decade-out bets. On humanoids: "Deployable dexterity will remain pathetic compared to human hands beyond 2036," and without new mechanical systems, walking humanoids stay too unsafe to be near real humans. On self-driving: only Waymo and Zoox matter in the US, with human intervention rate as the metric that decides profitability. On quantum: the winners will be analog-style machines that emulate physical systems, not digital ones. On AI: today's neural nets rest on primitive 1960s-era neuron models, and LLMs need to be "boxed in" to be safe rather than just trained more.

- "Why Today's Humanoids Won't Learn Dexterity" / humanoid critique (within the 2026 scorecard, 2025-2026), https://rodneybrooks.com/predictions-scorecard-2026-january-01/
  His sharpest current argument. He says training regimes for locomotion and dexterity are "ignoring or trying to zero out the embodiment of physical robots," reducing them to "apps with legs and arms, characters in video games." He notes the danger from a walking biped scales roughly with the cube of its weight, which forces makers to go lightweight, which then kills the payload and the elder-care use case they are selling. His closing line: "Humanoid romanticism may not be our future after all."

- "My Dated Predictions" (original Jan 1, 2018, foundational reference), https://rodneybrooks.com/my-dated-predictions/
  The base document the annual scorecards grade against. It lays out NET (no earlier than) dates across self-driving cars, robotics/AI, and human spaceflight to Mars. Useful as a calibration tool: a public, falsifiable, decades-long set of predictions from an insider who is structurally biased toward deflating hype.

### Hooks for the brain
- Brooks is the perfect counterweight character in any "robots are coming" piece. The Futures Brain needs at least one credible bear, and his is the most rigorous.
- The "different speeds" framework (research speed vs hype speed vs deployment speed vs economic-change speed) is a reusable mental model for evaluating any breathless tech claim, including AI marketing tools.
- His "hemming and hawing" / humans-secretly-in-the-loop category is a great lens for auditing demos and product launches. Good basis for a "how to tell a real product from a staged demo" post.
- His public, dated, self-graded scorecard is a rare honesty mechanism worth imitating. A "score your own predictions every year" angle for builders.
- Contrarian pairing: read him directly against Adcock and Musk on humanoids. The disagreement is concrete and dated, so it can be tracked.

## 82. Eric Jang

#source
Domain: Robotics and space
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://evjang.com/
- Blog: https://evjang.com/ (the homepage is the post index)
- RSS: https://evjang.com/feed.xml (verified, working Jekyll feed)
- X: https://x.com/ericjang11 (handle @ericjang11, confirmed)
- Podcast / other: book "AI is Good for You" https://evjang.com/book/, talks https://evjang.com/talks/, GitHub https://github.com/ericjang, LinkedIn https://www.linkedin.com/in/evjang
- Paywall: no

### Who they are
Robot learning researcher and writer. Former VP of AI at 1X Technologies (humanoid robots) from 2022 to early 2026, and before that a research scientist at Google Brain. As of early 2026 he left 1X and is taking time to study and recalibrate before his next role.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Disembodied AI and robotic AI will arrive at roughly the same time because the hard engineering problems are the same, so "all roads lead to robotics" and the whole economy is reorganizing around scaling machine intelligence into the physical world.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "All Roads Lead to Robotics" (Mar 3, 2024), https://evjang.com/2024/03/03/all-roads-robots.html
  His signature thesis. Jang argues that the engineering and infrastructure problems in large language models are essentially the same as those in robotics, so disembodied AGI and robotic AGI should happen at roughly the same time, not decades apart. As software agents interact with the messy real world (support, logistics, ride-sharing), they hit the same entropy and reliability problems robots already face. His conclusion: robotics is not a niche, it is where most software is heading.

- "As Rocks May Think" (Feb 4, 2026), https://evjang.com/2026/02/04/rocks.html
  A riff on Vannevar Bush's 1945 "As We May Think." Core idea: we have taught rocks (silicon) to think and they are getting very smart, which will create astronomical demand for inference compute, much like air conditioning unlocked productivity once it was cheap. He pictures a near future of ambient, always-on machine "thinkers" constantly re-planning deadlines, paying down tech debt, and guiding business decisions in real time. It is a strong "what does abundant cheap cognition do to the economy" piece.

- "Leaving 1X" (Jan 21, 2026), https://evjang.com/2026/01/21/leaving-1x.html
  His reflection on four years building robot learning at a leading humanoid company. He notes the Overton window on general-purpose robotics has shifted, meaning ideas once seen as fringe are now mainstream. He describes stepping back to "empty his cup of priors," re-implement papers, and catch up on the frontier. Useful as an honest insider snapshot of where humanoid robot learning actually stands in 2026.

- "Science and Engineering for Learned Robots" (Mar 14, 2021), https://evjang.com/2021/03/14/learning-robots.html
  An earlier but still-cited framing of how to actually make progress in robot learning. He distinguishes the science (understanding generalization) from the engineering (building reliable data and infrastructure pipelines) and argues the field is bottlenecked more by engineering discipline than by clever ideas. Foundational for understanding his "data and scale beat cleverness" worldview, which echoes the LLM scaling story.

### Hooks for the brain
- The "all roads lead to robotics" thesis is a clean bridge between the AI software story and the physical robot story. It lets the Futures Brain connect a marketer-facing AI tool argument to the bigger embodiment trend.
- "As Rocks May Think" gives a vivid, ownable metaphor (ambient always-on machine thinkers) for cheap cognition. Excellent raw material for a forward-looking essay about how work changes when thinking is nearly free.
- Jang is a rare practitioner-writer: he was inside a top humanoid company and writes plainly. He is a credible middle voice between Brooks (bear) and Adcock or Musk (bull).
- Watch his next move after 1X. Where a respected robot-learning lead goes next is itself a signal about which approach (humanoids, special-purpose, foundation models for action) is winning.
- His book "AI is Good for You" is an optimistic counter-narrative to AI doom. Worth mining for a balanced "is this good for us" angle.

- Paywall: partial (Ars Technica is mostly free with ads; some access and the newsletter are gated for subscribers)

### Who they are
Senior space editor at Ars Technica and a meteorologist by training. The most-read independent reporter on the commercial launch industry, best known for the weekly Rocket Report and for deeply sourced books on SpaceX's early history.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Cut through the hype and vaporware to show what the launch and spaceflight industry can actually do, tracking the real shift from government programs toward commercial, reusable, high-cadence space access.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Rocket Report" weekly newsletter (ongoing, every Friday), https://arstechnica.com/tag/rocket-report/ (URL unverified)
  His flagship running feature, tagged "Amid the hype and vaporware, we're bringing clarity to the launch industry." Each issue rounds up developments across small and large launch, from SpaceX to startups to national programs, with skeptical, sourced commentary. It is the single best ongoing pulse-check on where launch is actually heading, as opposed to where founders say it is heading. For the Futures Brain it is a recurring fact-check layer on space hype.

- "Reentry: SpaceX, Elon Musk, and the Reusable Rockets that Launched a Second Space Age" (2024 book), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Berger_(journalist)
  His deeply reported history of the Falcon 9 and Dragon era, the period that made reusable orbital rockets normal. It documents how reusability went from industry punchline to the foundation of modern spaceflight economics. Essential background for understanding why launch costs are falling and what that unlocks for the next 10 to 20 years.

- "Liftoff: Elon Musk and the Desperate Early Days that Launched SpaceX" (2021 book), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Berger_(journalist)
  The prequel, on SpaceX's near-death early years developing Falcon 1. It is the origin story of the company now reshaping space, and a case study in how a commercial newcomer overturned a government-dominated industry. Useful for grounding any forward-looking take on private space in how fragile and contingent the breakthrough actually was.

- Ongoing SpaceX, NASA, and Starship coverage at Ars Technica (continuous), https://muckrack.com/sciguyspace/articles
  His running reporting on Starship test campaigns, NASA Artemis and policy, and the commercial space race. He is often first and most accurate on schedule slips and technical setbacks, which makes him the corrective to founder timelines (including Musk's). His Muck Rack archive is the most reliable index of his latest pieces given the main site's access limits.

### Hooks for the brain
- Berger is the designated fact-checker node of the whole robotics-and-space map. Use his reporting to sanity-check Musk and Beck timelines before the brain treats any date as real.
- The Rocket Report's "hype and vaporware vs clarity" stance is a ready-made editorial posture. A "what the launch industry actually shipped this week" angle, translated for a general audience, is reusable content.
- His arc from meteorologist to top space journalist, plus running Space City Weather on the side, is a niche-authority builder story that fits a maker audience.
- His books trace the reusability revolution that drives falling launch costs. That cost curve is the hidden engine behind nearly every optimistic 10-to-20-year space claim, so it is worth a dedicated explainer.
