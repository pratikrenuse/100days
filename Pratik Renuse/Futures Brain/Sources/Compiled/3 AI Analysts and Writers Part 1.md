### Who they are
Ben Thompson writes Stratechery, the most influential subscription newsletter on tech business and strategy. Solo independent analyst, originator of the Aggregation Theory framework, now focused heavily on AI's strategic and economic impact.

### Their future thesis (one line)
AI agents (not just chatbots) are reshaping the demand for compute and the structure of the tech value chain: integration between model and harness will decide who captures profit, and the economic pressure to replace human "cogs" with agents will be impossible for enterprises to resist.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "AI and the Human Condition" (January 5, 2026), https://stratechery.com/2026/ai-and-the-human-condition/
  A free rebuttal to Dwarkesh Patel and Philip Trammell's "Capital in the 22nd Century." Thompson argues that even if AI can do all human jobs, people will still prefer human-made content and human connection, so an economy for human labor persists. He uses the collapse of US farm employment (81% in 1810 to about 1% today) as evidence that humans keep inventing new valuable work, and casts publications like Stratechery as community "totem poles" that AI-generated, individualized content cannot replace.

- "Agents Over Bubbles" (March 16, 2026), https://stratechery.com/2026/agents-over-bubbles/
  Thompson reverses his prior "we may be in a bubble" stance and argues the AI capex boom is justified, not speculative. He frames three LLM inflection points (ChatGPT, OpenAI o1 reasoning, and agentic harnesses like Claude Code with Opus 4.5) and contends agents massively increase compute demand while needing only a small number of high-agency operators to drive huge economic impact. He predicts a wave of AI-attributed layoffs as companies "rightsize" toward much smaller agent-augmented workforces.

- "AI Power, Now and In 100 Years" (2026), https://stratechery.com/2026/ai-power-now-and-in-100-years/
  A longer-horizon piece on how AI shifts power dynamics across the economy and society over a century-scale timeframe. (Capture the free framing; full text may sit behind Stratechery Plus.)

- "The Benefits of Bubbles" (November 5, 2025), https://stratechery.com/2025/the-benefits-of-bubbles/
  Argues that even if AI spending is a bubble, bubbles can be socially productive by funding overbuilt infrastructure that later gets used cheaply (railways, fiber). Useful as the counterweight he later partly retracts in "Agents Over Bubbles."

### Hooks for the brain
- The "agency narrowing" thesis: AI impact does not require mass adoption, just a few high-agency operators each directing many agents. Sharp counter to "everyone must adopt AI" marketing narratives.
- Integration vs modularity as the profit predictor: models commoditize, harnesses differentiate. A clean lens for evaluating which AI companies survive (he bets on Anthropic and OpenAI as integration points).
- Hidden player: the "harness" layer (Claude Code, Codex, Copilot Cowork) as the real moat, not the model. Underrated angle for marketers thinking about AI tooling.
- Thompson openly disagrees with Dwarkesh Patel on whether humans stay economically necessary: a built-in debate to mine for the brain.

### Who they are
Nathan Benaich is founder and General Partner of Air Street Capital, a London VC firm investing in AI-first companies. He is the lead author of the annual State of AI Report, the most widely read independent review of AI progress, published every year since 2018.

### Their future thesis (one line)
AI has entered its industrial era: compute, energy, and geopolitics now determine the frontier, capability-per-dollar roughly doubles every few months, China has become a credible number two, and value increasingly accrues to those who run AI at scale rather than those who merely build models.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The State of AI Report 2025" (October 9, 2025), https://press.airstreet.com/p/the-state-of-ai-2025 (full report at https://stateof.ai)
  The eighth annual report, covering research, industry, politics, and safety, plus a first-ever survey of 1,200 practitioners. Headline reads: the US holds the frontier but China (DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi) is within points on reasoning and coding, capability-per-dollar is doubling every 3 to 6 months, 44% of US businesses now pay for AI (up from 5% in 2023), and "Stargate"-scale clusters mark the industrial era of AI. Notes safety budgets are tiny (all 11 major US safety orgs spend about $133M a year, less than a frontier lab burns in a day).

- State of AI Report 2025 Predictions section (October 2025), within https://stateof.ai
  Benaich scores the prior year at 5/10 and issues ten new forward predictions, including: a Chinese lab tops a global leaderboard, AI agents make a real scientific discovery, datacenter NIMBYism hits US elections, and Trump moves to ban state AI laws. A clean, datable set of testable futures bets.

- "2025 Year in Review" (Air Street Capital), https://press.airstreet.com/p/2025-review
  Air Street's annual reflection on the AI investment landscape, tying the firm's portfolio theses to the macro shifts captured in the report. Useful for the investor's-eye view of where capital is flowing.

- State of AI Usage Survey 2025 (October 2025), https://www.stateof.ai/survey
  First large open-access survey of 1,200 AI practitioners: 95% use AI at work or home, 76% pay out of pocket, with rising spend and broad reported productivity gains. A demand-side data anchor for the "is adoption real" question.

### Hooks for the brain
- The State of AI Report is itself a recurring "futures artifact": every October it ships datable predictions and grades the prior year. Ideal spine for a year-over-year tracker.
- "RIP AGI, long live Superintelligence": Benaich flags the deliberate rebranding of the AI mission as a marketing move. Sharp angle on how labs frame the future.
- Jevons paradox on compute: cheaper training runs drive more runs and more total compute, so efficiency gains increase demand. Counterintuitive thesis worth a standalone post.
- Hidden players: open-weights ecosystem shift from Meta's Llama to China's Qwen (40% of new Hugging Face fine-tunes), and sovereign AI funds (UAE MGX, China Big Fund) as future geopolitical actors.

### Who they are
Ethan Mollick is a professor at the Wharton School (University of Pennsylvania) studying entrepreneurship, innovation, and AI, and co-director of the Generative AI Labs at Wharton. Through One Useful Thing he is one of the most-read practical explainers of what AI means for work, education, and life, and author of the NYT bestseller "Co-Intelligence."

### Their future thesis (one line)
AI improves along a "jagged frontier" (superhuman at some tasks, weak at others, hard to predict), and the "bitter lesson" is now reaching business: general-purpose models trained on outcomes will increasingly displace handcrafted, process-specific solutions, so organizations should define good outputs and let AI find its own path through the mess.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Bitter Lesson versus The Garbage Can" (July 28, 2025), https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-bitter-lesson-versus-the-garbage
  Combines Richard Sutton's "bitter lesson" (brute-force general AI beats hand-coded human expertise) with the organizational "garbage can" model (companies are messy, undocumented systems). Mollick argues outcome-trained agents like ChatGPT agent may navigate organizational chaos without ever mapping it, which could make a company's hard-won process knowledge a depreciating asset. The open question he poses: are organizations chess games that yield to compute, or something fundamentally messier.

- "Prophecies of the Flood" (January 10, 2025), https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/prophecies-of-the-flood
  Surveys the sudden urgency among frontier-lab researchers about near-term superintelligence, arguing a "flood of intelligence" may arrive imminently rather than in decades. A useful snapshot of how seriously insiders take fast timelines, written for a general audience.

- "On Working with Wizards" (September 11, 2025), https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/on-working-with-wizards
  Using GPT-5 Pro as the example, Mollick describes a shift from "co-intelligence" (humans shaping the process collaboratively) to working with a "wizard" that simply hands you a finished, opaque output. Flags the loss of human oversight and process visibility as models get more capable, a key tension for the future of knowledge work.

- "The Shape of AI: Jaggedness, Bottlenecks and Salients" (2026), https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-shape-of-ai-jaggedness-bottlenecks
  Develops the jagged-frontier idea into a strategic map: as AI capability crosses thresholds it unlocks radical new use cases unevenly, reshaping product strategy, talent mix, and org design. Argues recursive self-improvement is now an explicit roadmap item at every major lab, producing a rolling, unpredictable environment.

### Hooks for the brain
- The "jagged frontier": AI is unpredictably good and bad task-by-task, so you cannot reason about exposure top-down. Direct, practical framing for marketers deciding what to automate.
- "Define the output, not the process": the bitter lesson applied to business automation. A reframe that undercuts most process-mapping consulting.
- "Co-intelligence" to "wizard" arc: as models get stronger, humans drift from collaborators to supplicants. Rich angle on what to preserve in human-in-the-loop design.
- Mollick writes everything himself and only uses AI for editing, then says so explicitly. Credibility hook for the "human-authored in an AI world" theme.

### Their future thesis (one line)
The future of AI capability runs through post-training and reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards, reasoning models will generalize beyond narrow benchmarks, and the strategic battle that matters is keeping a genuinely open model ecosystem alive (especially in the US) so frontier capability is not monopolized by a few closed labs.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Why Reasoning Models will Generalize" (January 2025), https://www.interconnects.ai/p/why-reasoning-models-will-generalize
  Argues that reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks will not stay confined to math and code but will transfer to broader domains. A foundational forward claim about where model capability is heading, from someone who trains these systems.

- "Scaling Realities" (November 2024), https://www.interconnects.ai/p/scaling-realities
  A grounded take on what scaling can and cannot deliver, written against hype, separating durable trends from marketing. Useful as a sober counterweight to pure scaling-maximalism.

- "Farewell Ai2" (2026), https://www.interconnects.ai/p/farewell-ai2
  Lambert's reflection on leaving the Allen Institute after nearly three years building Olmo and Tulu, laying out his three forward goals: provide clarity on frontier-model evolution, build a vibrant and diverse open-model ecosystem, and create institutions that sustain those goals. The clearest statement of his personal mission and where he is taking it next.

- "AGI is What You Want it to Be" (April 2024), https://www.interconnects.ai/p/agi-is-what-you-want-it-to-be
  Deconstructs "AGI" as an under-defined, motivated term whose meaning shifts with the speaker's incentives. A clarifying lens for reading every lab's future claims.

### Hooks for the brain
- RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) as the engine behind reasoning models: a technical thesis with big downstream implications for which tasks AI conquers next.
- The open vs closed model fight as a future-of-power story: Lambert is building The ATOM Project, a coalition for truly open American models. Hidden-player angle on who controls frontier AI.
- "AGI is whatever you want it to be": a debunking frame for cutting through lab marketing about timelines.
- He writes from inside the lab with no LLM-generated prose, and discloses paid advisory roles (Mercor, Arcee AI). Strong credibility signal for a "trustworthy insider" source.

### Hooks for the brain

- Prompt injection and the "lethal trifecta" (Willison's term for combining private data access, untrusted content, and external communication): an under-covered security future that marketers deploying AI tools should understand.

### Who they are
Gwern Branwen is a pseudonymous independent researcher and writer who has published long-form essays at gwern.net for over a decade on AI, statistics, psychology, and more. He was one of the earliest and most influential proponents of the AI scaling hypothesis, and is known for unusually rigorous, heavily cited, continuously updated work.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Intelligence is largely a product of scale: keep scaling compute, data, and model size and capabilities keep emerging, so much of AI progress comes from quantitative scaling rather than new algorithms, and the deep-learning revolution is unfolding roughly as the scaling hypothesis predicted.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Scaling Hypothesis" (published May 28, 2020; updated through 2022), https://gwern.net/scaling-hypothesis
  The essay that made the scaling argument legible to a wide audience, written in the wake of GPT-3. Argues that neural nets absorb data and compute to generalize and manifest new abilities even at scales that are trivial by global standards, and that the path to very capable AI is mostly "just" more scale. Remains a foundational reference for fast-timeline AI thinking.

- "AI Cannibalism Can Be Good" (2025), https://gwern.net/ (in the AI scaling directory: https://gwern.net/doc/ai/scaling/index)
  Pushes back on the common fear that training models on AI-generated ("synthetic") data inevitably causes model collapse, arguing that under the right conditions models learning from model outputs can be beneficial rather than degrading. A contrarian forward take on the data-supply question for future training.

- "AI scaling" directory (continuously updated), https://gwern.net/doc/ai/scaling/index
  Gwern's curated, annotated collection of scaling research and his own notes, including 2026 entries. Functions as a living bibliography of where scaling evidence is heading; treat it as a tracker rather than a single dated piece.

- "AI economics" directory (continuously updated), https://gwern.net/doc/ai/scaling/economics/index
  Companion collection focused on the economics of AI scaling: compute costs, returns, and the macro implications of cheap intelligence. Useful for the "what does scaling do to the economy" question.

### Hooks for the brain
- The scaling hypothesis is the load-bearing idea behind most fast-AI-timeline forecasting. Gwern is the canonical source; cite him when the brain needs the origin of "just scale it."
- "Synthetic data need not cause collapse": a contrarian thesis that reframes the looming data-shortage debate. Strong standalone post angle.
- Hidden-player credibility story: an anonymous writer who called the AI trajectory early while living on a very low income (per the Dwarkesh interview). A memorable "the future was visible to outsiders" narrative.
- Everything is CC0 public domain and continuously revised: a model for how durable futures knowledge can be maintained, not just published once.

### Who they are

As of early 2026 he had published around 115 episodes to 60,000-plus subscribers, and co-authored the 2025 book "The Scaling Era."

### Their future thesis (one line)
Scaling compute has driven the current AI boom, but true human-level AGI is likely still 10 to 20 years away because today's models embed human knowledge rather than learning continuously from experience, so the next era is as much about new research (continual learning, agency) as about more scale.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- Ilya Sutskever interview (second appearance) (November 25, 2025), https://www.dwarkesh.com/ (episode page on the Dwarkesh Podcast)
  Sutskever frames 2012 to 2020 as the age of research, 2020 to 2025 as the age of scaling, and 2026 onward as a return to research. He gives a 5-to-20-year range for AI that learns like a human and then scales past us. A high-signal statement of timelines from one of the field's most important figures.

- Richard Sutton interview (September 2025), https://www.dwarkesh.com/ (episode page on the Dwarkesh Podcast)
  Sutton (the "bitter lesson" author and reinforcement-learning pioneer) argues current LLMs merely embed human knowledge rather than learning from experience, and that history shows such approaches eventually get superseded. The episode crystallizes the central debate about whether scaling LLMs is the road to AGI or a detour.

- "The Scaling Era: An Oral History of AI, 2019 to 2025" (book, 2025), https://www.dwarkesh.com/ (overview and excerpts on the site)
  Curated from his interviews, this is the first comprehensive oral-history portrait of the scaling era, from how LLMs work to the possibility of AI takeover or explosive economic growth. A durable reference for how the people building AI described the moment as it happened.

- Dylan Patel (SemiAnalysis) interview on compute bottlenecks (March 2026), https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dylan-patel
  A deep dive into the three big bottlenecks to scaling AI compute (power, chips, and data-center buildout). Grounds the abstract scaling debate in the hard physical and supply-chain constraints that will shape the next several years.

### Hooks for the brain
- The scaling-vs-research debate runs through his guest list: Sutskever (5 to 20 years), Sutton (LLMs are a detour), Patel disagreeing with both. A ready-made "experts disagree on AGI" map.
- Dwarkesh's own view: AGI is 10 to 20 years out, gated by continual learning and agency, not just compute. Directly on-thesis for a 10-to-20-year futures brain.
- Compute bottlenecks (power, chips, data centers) as the real near-term constraint: the Dylan Patel episode is a hidden-infrastructure angle.
- The podcast is a primary-source pipeline: nearly every major AI figure (Hassabis, Amodei, Huang, Karpathy, Nadella) goes on the record about the future here. Treat it as a sourcing engine for the brain.

### Their future thesis (one line)
We live in an "exponential age" where AI and related technologies compound faster than institutions can adapt (the "exponential gap"), and AI's compute demands have now broken the 50-year Moore's Law trend, reshaping the economy, energy systems, and the balance of power.

- "The next 24 months in AI" (2026), https://www.exponentialview.co/p/the-next-24-months-in-ai

  Described as the first research report to dissect the demand side of the AI economy, reporting roughly $110B in generative-AI sales over the trailing 12 months and a run rate above $175B annualized, with executives across the US and Europe planning to invest more. - "The year ahead" (annual), https://www.exponentialview.co/p/the-year-ahead
  Azhar's recurring start-of-year scan of the trends he expects to matter most across AI and exponential technologies. A reliable annual futures artifact for the brain's year-over-year spine.

### Hooks for the brain
