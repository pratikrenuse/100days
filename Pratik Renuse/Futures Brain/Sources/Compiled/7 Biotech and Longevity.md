## 84. David Sinclair

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://sinclair.hms.harvard.edu (The Sinclair Lab, Harvard Medical School)
- Blog: No standalone text blog. Primary output is the Lifespan podcast and X.
- RSS: unverified (no personal blog feed found)

- Paywall: no (podcast and X are free; some lab papers are journal-gated)

### Who they are
Tenured professor of genetics at Harvard Medical School and co-director of the Paul F. Glenn Center for Biology of Aging Research. He is the field's highest-profile and most polarizing voice, author of Lifespan (2019), and co-founder of Life Biosciences.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Aging is a loss of epigenetic information that can be reversed, and within a decade a doctor could prescribe a reprogramming drug that makes the whole body measurably years younger.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "David Sinclair plans to test whole-body rejuvenation drugs in the XPrize competition" (June 9, 2026), https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/06/09/1138545/david-sinclair-plans-to-test-whole-body-rejuvenation-drugs-in-the-xprize-competition/
  Sinclair confirmed plans to give volunteers an oral "chemical reprogramming" cocktail, code-named SL-100, as part of the $101M XPRIZE Healthspan competition, which rewards teams that can restore a person to a 10-years-younger state across immune, cognitive, and muscle measures. Chemical reprogramming uses small molecules to mimic the embryonic reprogramming genes, with the advantage that drugs travel the bloodstream and can reach the whole body, unlike gene therapy. Other scientists are skeptical: rival labs report whole-animal chemical rejuvenation has so far been toxic or ineffective, and the data behind SL-100 is unpublished. The piece doubles as a map of the field, naming XPRIZE finalists due August 2026 and the standardization-of-aging-measurement problem as the real bottleneck.
- "The first human test of a rejuvenation method will begin shortly" (Life Biosciences trial, January 27, 2026), https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/01/27/1131796/the-first-human-test-of-a-rejuvenation-method-will-begin-shortly/
  Life Biosciences, co-founded by Sinclair, won approval for a first-in-human Phase 1 trial of partial epigenetic reprogramming using three Yamanaka factors delivered to the eye, aiming to restore vision in glaucoma and NAION. This is the first time partial reprogramming has moved from animals into a regulated human trial. The eye is the proving ground because it is contained and observable; success would be the strongest signal yet that resetting epigenetic marks can rejuvenate human tissue. The company announced it treated its first patient in June 2026.
- "Lifespan: Why We Age and Why We Don't Have To" (2019, ongoing reference), https://www.lifespanpodcast.com
  Sinclair's book set out his Information Theory of Aging: aging is driven by the cell losing track of which genes to express (epigenetic noise) rather than by DNA damage itself. It popularized partial cellular reprogramming and reframed aging as a treatable condition rather than an inevitability. The thesis remains the intellectual backbone of his current drug and gene-therapy bets, so it is the canonical starting text for his worldview.

### Hooks for the brain
- Hidden player surfaced: NewLimit (Brian Armstrong and Jacob Kimmel) raised a further $435M on June 2, 2026, from Founders Fund, pursuing liver "age reprogramming." This is person #91 in this list, so the Sinclair and Kimmel dossiers cross-reference into a single "reprogramming race" thesis.
- Credibility split is the story: Sinclair retains his Harvard chair but resigned the presidency of the Academy for Health and Lifespan Research in March 2024 after the dog-supplement claim drew public rebuke from peers. A Futures Brain post could separate "the reprogramming science is real and advancing" from "Sinclair the promoter overclaims," because both are true.
- The XPRIZE Healthspan competition (Peter Diamandis, funded by Saudi Arabia's Hevolution) is itself a future-shaping institution: its real output may be standardized regulator-grade aging biomarkers, the thing that lets the FDA ever approve an anti-aging drug. Worth its own node.
- Watch SL-100, AI-designed reprogramming cocktails, and the August 2026 XPRIZE finalist list as concrete 12-month tripwires.

## 85. Peter Attia

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity

- Paywall: partial (podcast and weekly newsletter are free; deep show notes and premium articles are members-only)

### Who they are
Stanford, Johns Hopkins, and NIH-trained physician, founder of the medical practice Early Medical, host of The Peter Attia Drive, and author of the number one bestseller Outlive (2023). He is the leading popularizer of preventive "Medicine 3.0."

### Their future thesis (one line)
The next era of medicine shifts from treating disease late to delaying or preventing the big four killers decades early, using better biomarkers, GLP-1 and rapamycin-class drugs, exercise as medicine, and increasingly AI-driven personalization.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Outlive: The Science and Art of Longevity" (March 28, 2023), https://peterattiamd.com/outlive/
  Outlive lays out Attia's Medicine 3.0 thesis: rather than waiting for heart disease, cancer, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disease to appear, medicine should target their root drivers decades in advance. The book is the clearest statement of his framework and remains the reference text for how a mainstream-credible doctor thinks about extending healthspan, not just lifespan. It treats exercise, sleep, nutrition, and emotional health as primary interventions alongside drugs.
- "A new era of longevity science" (The Drive, AMA / episode, 2026), https://peterattiamd.com/podcast/
  Recent episodes argue current biological aging clocks are not yet clinically useful and that the field needs more actionable biomarkers before anti-aging drugs can be deployed responsibly. Attia tracks rapamycin dosing trials, GLP-1 receptor agonists and their effects far beyond weight loss, SGLT2 inhibitors, and compounds like urolithin A and NAD boosters with a deliberately skeptical eye. The throughline is that AI plus rising funding could accelerate aging breakthroughs, but the data bar must stay high.
- "GLP-1 receptor agonists and muscle loss" (The Drive, AMA #86, June 29, 2026), https://peterattiamd.com/podcast/
  Attia examines new data on preserving lean muscle while using GLP-1 drugs for weight loss, a fast-emerging clinical problem as these medicines go mainstream. He frames muscle as a longevity organ and resistance training plus protein as the countermeasure. This is a concrete example of his method: take a hyped new drug class and pressure-test the second-order effects for healthspan.

### Hooks for the brain
- Attia is the credibility anchor of consumer longevity. Where Sinclair makes bold reprogramming claims, Attia is the measured clinician who tells you which interventions have evidence today. A Futures Brain piece could pair them as the two poles of the longevity conversation: promise versus proof.
- GLP-1 drugs are his recurring future theme and may be the single biggest near-term longevity story (effects on heart, brain, addiction, inflammation, muscle). Worth its own node, with Attia as a primary skeptical source.
- "Medicine 3.0" is a clean, ownable framing for the shift from reactive to predictive and preventive medicine. Good spine for a post on where clinical practice heads by 2040.
- Watch for whether aging biomarkers ever become clinically actionable. Attia treats this as the unlock that gates the whole anti-aging drug field, the same bottleneck the XPRIZE Healthspan competition is trying to solve.

## 86. Derek Lowe

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.science.org/content/author/derek-lowe (author page)
- Blog: In the Pipeline, https://www.science.org/blogs/pipeline
- RSS: https://www.science.org/digital-feed/pipeline

- Podcast / other: Frequent podcast guest, for example The External Medicine Podcast and Disrupted Science; also writes for Chemistry World
- Paywall: no (In the Pipeline is editorially independent and free on Science.org; full content is Lowe's own)

### Who they are
Veteran medicinal chemist with more than 30 years in pharma drug discovery, and author of In the Pipeline since 2002, the most widely read working-chemist's blog on drug development. He is the field's reality check on hype, and an unusually clear explainer of why most drugs fail.

### Their future thesis (one line)
He is a short-term pessimist and long-term optimist on AI in drug discovery: the hardest problems (picking the right target, predicting human toxicity) are exactly the ones AI cannot yet solve, but there is no law of physics stopping it, so the gains will be real, uneven, and slower than the hype.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Derek Lowe on AI in Drug Discovery: Between Hype and Hope" (April 15, 2025), https://www.bio-itworld.com/news/2025/04/15/derek-lowe-on-ai-in-drug-discovery-between-hype-and-hope
  Lowe argues the drug industry's central problem is an 85% clinical failure rate driven by a lack of predictive insight, not a lack of effort, and that AI is weakest precisely where the field needs it most: target selection and human toxicity. He notes the medicinal-chemistry literature is inconsistent and often wrong, so models trained on it inherit that noise. Still, he sees genuine roles for AI in well-defined discovery-stage tasks and insists the long-term ceiling is high. The piece is the cleanest summary of his calibrated, evidence-first stance.
- "In the Pipeline" ongoing AI-and-chemistry coverage (2025 to 2026), https://www.science.org/blogs/pipeline
  Through 2025 and into 2026 Lowe has been working through AI-assisted chemistry papers, including agentic systems like "Co-Scientist" that propose hypotheses and mechanisms for biomedical discovery. He repeatedly tests grand claims against what actually replicates in the lab, which is the value of reading him in near real time. For a futures brain, his blog is the best running ground truth on whether AI drug discovery is delivering. New posts appear several times a week.
- Lowe's skeptical commentary on AlphaFold and protein-structure hype (recurring theme), https://www.science.org/blogs/pipeline
  Lowe has consistently argued that solving protein structures, while remarkable, does not by itself revolutionize drug discovery, because knowing a structure is far from knowing how to drug it safely in humans. This is his signature move: separating a real scientific advance from the inflated commercial promise built on top of it. It is a useful corrective when assessing any "AI solved biology" headline.

### Hooks for the brain
- Lowe is the single best skeptic source for the Futures Brain. When any AI-in-bio or longevity claim looks too clean, his blog is where to check whether working scientists buy it. Treat In the Pipeline as a built-in fact-check feed.
- His "short-term pessimist, long-term optimist" framing is a reusable lens for almost every frontier-tech claim, not just pharma. Good spine for a post on how to read hype cycles.
- He is the human counterweight to the Arc Institute and Manas AI optimism in this very list. A strong post would put Hsu or Mukherjee's "AI rewrites biology" against Lowe's "the literature is a mess and toxicity is unsolved" and let the reader hold both.
- The 85% clinical failure rate and the unsolved problems of target selection and human toxicity prediction are the real bottlenecks of the next decade. Lowe names them precisely. Worth a dedicated node on "what AI must crack before drug discovery actually speeds up."

Cardiologist and executive vice president of Scripps Research, founder and director of the Scripps Research Translational Institute, and one of medicine's leading voices on AI and digital health. He authored Deep Medicine (2019) and Super Agers (2025), and in March 2026 joined Flagship Pioneering as academic advisor on preemptive health.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Medicine is shifting from reactive sick care to preemptive, data-rich, AI-augmented health, where multimodal AI, omics, and continuous sensors let us predict and prevent disease and extend healthspan years before symptoms appear.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Super Agers: An Evidence-Based Approach to Longevity" (2025), https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Super-Agers/Eric-Topol/9781668067666
  Topol's book argues healthspan extension will come from five converging forces: lifestyle, cellular science, omics, AI, and new drugs and vaccines. He documents how AI is transforming cancer and cardiovascular screening and treats GLP-1 drugs as a turning point whose benefits reach far beyond weight and diabetes. The evidence-first framing positions him as the credible, data-driven counterpart to hype-heavy longevity figures.   Topol analyzes a shift from AI that assists with narrow tasks to autonomous agentic systems (citing Nature work on MIRA and AMIE) that can carry an end-to-end care plan. He argues this is the most consequential near-term change in clinical medicine and weighs both the promise and the risks of handing more of the workflow to AI. The piece is a clear marker of where he thinks frontline care is heading this decade.

  A conversation with oncologist-author Siddhartha Mukherjee on how AI will reshape drug discovery, longevity, and the idea of a continuously monitored "digital" patient. The two map where biomedicine is heading as AI compresses discovery timelines and data becomes the substrate of care. Useful as a two-expert synthesis of the AI-plus-medicine future, and it links directly to person #90 in this list.

### Hooks for the brain

- "Preemptive medicine" (his Flagship role) is a sharp, ownable frame for the predictive-prevention shift, parallel to Attia's "Medicine 3.0." A post could compare the two framings and who is building toward them.

## 88. Patrick Hsu

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://patrickhsu.com
- Blog: Arc Institute news and lab page, https://arcinstitute.org/labs/hsulab (no standalone personal blog; primary writing appears as Arc posts and papers)
- RSS: unverified (Arc newsletter via signup form; no public RSS feed confirmed)
- X: https://x.com/pdhsu (@pdhsu)
- Podcast / other: Frequent guest, for example Sequoia Training Data, https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/training-data-patrick-hsu/
- Paywall: no (Arc content and Evo models are open; some papers sit behind journal access)

### Who they are
Co-founder and Core Investigator of the Arc Institute and assistant professor of bioengineering at UC Berkeley, an early CRISPR-Cas pioneer now building AI foundation models for biology. Arc is an independent nonprofit in Palo Alto aiming to compress the timeline of basic biomedical research.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Biology is becoming an engineering discipline: AI models trained on the language of DNA and whole cells will let scientists read biological states and write new genomes in an iterative read-write-think loop, eventually simulating cells in software before touching a lab bench.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Evo 2: One Year Later" (March 4, 2026), https://arcinstitute.org/news/evo-2-one-year-later
  Marks the Nature publication of Evo 2, a DNA foundation model trained across all domains of life and the largest fully open biological AI model to date. Beyond predicting disease-causing mutations, it generated the first AI-designed, experimentally validated organism (a functional bacteriophage), pointing toward designer phages for antibiotic-resistant infections. Hsu frames Evo 2 as proof that generative genomics is real, with next steps to design entire collections of genes without specifying the rules, since the model has already learned them. The piece sketches a near future of read-write-think loops combining AI design with genome-engineering tools like bridge recombinases.
- "Arc's Virtual Cell Initiative" and the State / Stack models (2025 to 2026), https://arcinstitute.org/virtual-cell-initiative
  Arc's institute-wide effort to build virtual cell models that predict how cells respond to genetic, chemical, and environmental perturbations. State and Stack are early models trained on large perturbation datasets and single-cell data, with an annual Virtual Cell Challenge launched in 2025 to push the whole field. Hsu has predicted that by 2030 there will be virtual cell models accurate and useful enough to change how cell biology is done. This is the longest-horizon bet: simulating human biology in software.
- "An App Store for Biology with AI" (Sequoia Training Data interview, 2025), https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/training-data-patrick-hsu/
  Hsu lays out the vision of a biology lab without test tubes, where foundation models become a programmable layer others build on, like an app store. He connects the DNA-language work (Evo) and the cell-simulation work (virtual cells) into one stack for designing biology. Useful as the plain-language version of his thesis for a general audience.

### Hooks for the brain
- Arc Institute is itself a future-shaping institution: a nonprofit, no-grant-treadmill model funded to do high-risk biology fast, partnered with NVIDIA. The org structure may matter as much as any single result. Worth a node on "how science gets funded next."
- The open-model strategy is a deliberate counter to closed AI. Evo 2 has millions of API calls and tens of thousands of downloads. A post could contrast Arc's open biology models with proprietary efforts (for example OpenAI's longevity model, referenced in the Sinclair MIT Tech Review piece).
- Biosafety is the live tension: Arc excluded eukaryotic viruses from training and red-teamed the model. As biological foundation models get more capable, alignment and biosecurity become the gating issue. Strong future-risk angle.
- Cross-reference with Jacob Kimmel (NewLimit, #91): both are betting AI plus cell biology rewrites medicine, Hsu from open foundation models and Kimmel from reprogramming therapeutics.

### Who they are
Berlin-based scientist, author, and YouTuber, trained as a physicist at Oxford before switching to computational biology at the Francis Crick Institute. He wrote Ageless (2020) and co-founded and directs The Longevity Initiative, advocating for aging research as a public priority.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Aging is the single largest driver of disease and the biggest opportunity in medicine: if society funds it properly, we can build drugs that prevent many diseases at once rather than treating them one by one, the greatest revolution in medical history.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Ageless: The New Science of Getting Older Without Getting Old" (2020, ongoing reference), https://andrewsteele.co.uk/ageless/
  Steele's book is the accessible, evidence-first map of geroscience: senescent cells, telomeres, mitochondrial decline, and the case that targeting the aging process itself could prevent age-related diseases collectively. It is deliberately less hype-driven than celebrity longevity books, which makes it a reliable baseline for what the science actually supports. The core argument is that treating aging is both feasible and the highest-leverage health investment available.
- "Why even the healthiest people hit a wall at age 70" (Big Think interview, recent), https://bigthink.com/series/the-big-think-interview/andrew-steele-longevity-science/
  Steele explains why lifestyle alone plateaus and why biological interventions into aging itself are needed to push past current human limits. He frames the next era as moving from managing risk factors to repairing or slowing the underlying damage of aging. Useful as a concise statement of why he thinks geroscience, not just better habits, is the real frontier.
- "Andrew Steele's Occasional Updates" (Substack, ongoing), https://andrewsteele.substack.com
  His newsletter tracks developments in aging science, longevity policy, and his own work with The Longevity Initiative. It is the best place to follow his current thinking between books and videos. Steele uses it to translate new papers and funding news for a general audience and to argue for more public investment.

### Hooks for the brain
- Steele is the "sober realist" of the consumer longevity space: credentialed, cautious, and explicitly anti-hype. He is a useful counterweight when sourcing claims that Sinclair or supplement sellers overstate. Good for balance in any longevity post.
- The Longevity Initiative is a policy-and-advocacy angle, not a product. It frames aging research as a collective-action and funding problem, which is a distinct and underused thesis (most coverage is about companies and drugs). Worth a node on "why aging research is underfunded relative to its impact."
- He appeared in Netflix's "Don't Die" (the Bryan Johnson documentary), placing him inside the cultural longevity moment while keeping scientific distance. A post could use him to separate the science from the spectacle.
- His physics-to-biology path and machine-learning-on-NHS-records background make him a credible voice on data-driven prevention, a bridge to the AI-in-medicine theme shared with Topol and Hsu.

## 90. Siddhartha Mukherjee

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity

### Read them

- Blog: No standalone blog. Primary long-form output is books and New Yorker essays, https://www.newyorker.com/contributors/siddhartha-mukherjee

- X: https://x.com/DrSidMukherjee (@DrSidMukherjee)
- Podcast / other: Frequent guest, for example Eric Topol's Ground Truths; company sites Manas AI (https://manasai.co) and Immuneel
- Paywall: partial (New Yorker essays are partly paywalled; books are purchase)

### Who they are
Pulitzer-winning physician, oncologist, and author at Columbia University, known for The Emperor of All Maladies (2010), The Gene (2016), and The Song of the Cell (2022). In 2025 he co-founded the AI drug-discovery startup Manas AI with Reid Hoffman, and he was named to the 2026 TIME100 Health list.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Medicine is entering a "new human" era built on cells and genes as therapy, with AI compressing drug discovery and continuous data turning each patient into a monitored, predictable system, while the same tools raise hard questions about overdiagnosis and what counts as disease.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Song of the Cell: An Exploration of Medicine and the New Human" (2022, ongoing reference), https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Song-of-the-Cell/Siddhartha-Mukherjee/9781982117368
  Mukherjee traces medicine's shift from treating organs to engineering cells, covering CAR-T cancer therapies, stem cells, and the prospect of cellular and genetic engineering that could make hereditary disease a thing of the past. He frames the coming decades as the age of the "new human," where we build and deploy living cells as medicine. This is the canonical text for his view that cell and gene therapy, not just small molecules, define the future of treatment.
- "Manas AI launches to transform discovery of novel cancer medicines" (announced January to February 2025), https://manasai.co/news/manas-ai-launches-to-transform-discovery-of-novel-cancer-medicines
  Mukherjee and Reid Hoffman launched Manas AI with about $24.6M in seed funding to build a full-stack AI pipeline from target identification through clinical trials, starting with aggressive cancers like prostate, lymphoma, and triple-negative breast cancer. Mukherjee says the platform has virtually screened hundreds of thousands of candidate molecules, some chemically unimaginable before, with plans to reach millions. It is his concrete bet that generative chemistry plus biology will reshape how cancer drugs are found.
- "Early Warnings: New technologies promise to catch more cancers sooner, but such screening can pose hidden hazards" (The New Yorker, June 23, 2025), https://www.newyorker.com/contributors/siddhartha-mukherjee
  Mukherjee examines multi-cancer early-detection blood tests, which could revolutionize prevention, against their real risks: false positives, overdiagnosis, and unequal access. He argues that catching more cancers sooner is not automatically better and that the technology needs careful evidence before mass deployment. The essay is a model of how he holds promise and peril together, relevant to the whole "screen everything" future of medicine.

### Hooks for the brain
- Mukherjee is the rare figure who is both a frontline cancer doctor and a builder (Manas AI, Immuneel), which makes him a credible bridge between "AI will transform drug discovery" optimism and clinical reality. Strong anchor for an AI-plus-cancer node.
- His "new human" framing (cells and genes as medicine) pairs naturally with Patrick Hsu's generative-genomics work and the reprogramming companies in this list. A post could trace the arc from reading the genome (Evo) to writing cells as therapy (Song of the Cell) to AI-designed drugs (Manas).
- The 2025 updated edition of The Emperor of All Maladies added four chapters on detection, prevention, and treatment, a useful current snapshot of where oncology is heading. Worth flagging as a refreshed primary source.
- His New Yorker essays are the best place to watch him reason about the downside of new biotech (overdiagnosis, screening harms, equity). Good counterbalance to pure techno-optimism, and a recurring source for nuance.
- Cross-reference with Eric Topol (#87): their Ground Truths conversation on AI, longevity, and the "digital human" is a ready-made two-expert node.

## 91. Jacob Kimmel

#source
Domain: Biotech and longevity
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://newlimit.com (company); personal academic page https://jacobkimmel.com (unverified, listed in some bios)
- Blog: https://blog.newlimit.com (NewLimit Blog on Substack, authored by Kimmel)
- RSS: https://blog.newlimit.com/feed (Substack feed, standard but unverified for exact path)
- X: https://x.com/jacobkimmel (@jacobkimmel)
- Podcast / other: LinkedIn https://www.linkedin.com/in/jacob-kimmel/ ; guest on longevity and biotech podcasts
- Paywall: no (NewLimit blog posts are free; some prior academic papers are journal-gated)

### Who they are
Computational biologist, co-founder and CEO of NewLimit, the cellular reprogramming company he started in 2021 with Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong and bioengineer Blake Byers. NewLimit pairs machine learning with genomics to design medicines that reverse the age of cells.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Aging is plastic at the cellular level, so reprogramming old cells back to a youthful state could both treat diseases of aging and preserve health in nearly everyone, an opportunity orders of magnitude larger than treating one disease at a time.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "NewLimit raises $435M led by Founders Fund to bring longevity medicines to human trials" (June 2, 2026), https://blog.newlimit.com/p/newlimit-raises-435m-led-by-founders
  NewLimit closed a $435M Series C (reported valuation around $3.1B) led by Founders Fund, with Thrive, Greenoaks, Eli Lilly Ventures, and others. The money funds a first human trial, planned for next year, of a liver reprogramming therapy after a prototype reversed the age of old human liver cells. Kimmel says the company originally expected reprogramming to take more than a decade to reach humans but pulled the timeline forward on the strength of recent results. He frames aging medicines as potentially one of the few projects that benefit humanity for centuries.
- "How NewLimit discovers reprogramming medicines with AI and genomics" (NewLimit blog / technical posts, 2024 to 2026), https://blog.newlimit.com
  NewLimit's core method is to screen combinations of transcription factors at scale, read the effect on cells with single-cell genomics, and use machine learning to find factor sets that make old cells behave young without erasing cell identity. The same platform is being aimed across metabolic, vascular, and immune programs, with liver first. This is the clearest example of the "AI plus wet lab in a loop" model applied specifically to rejuvenation, distinct from a single hero molecule.
- Kimmel's prior academic work on partial reprogramming and cell-state machine learning (Calico and earlier, ongoing reference)
  Before NewLimit, Kimmel worked on quantitative models of cell state and aging, including time at Calico. That background frames NewLimit's bet: treat reprogramming as a controllable, measurable engineering problem rather than a vague rejuvenation promise.
