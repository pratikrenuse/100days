## 181. Erik Brynjolfsson

#source
Domain: Economics of AI; Director, Stanford Digital Economy Lab
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.brynjolfsson.com/
- Blog / where published: Stanford Digital Economy Lab (https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/), NBER, plus op-eds and journals; research list at https://www.brynjolfsson.com/research
- X / social: https://twitter.com/erikbryn (X), https://www.linkedin.com/in/erikbrynjolfsson/ (LinkedIn)
- Podcast / other: Frequent podcast and keynote guest (Microsoft WorkLab, a16z, EconTalk and others); represented for speaking by Stern Strategy
- Paywall: no (working papers and lab pages are free; some journal versions and FT/HBR coverage sit behind paywalls)

### Who they are
Jerry Yang and Akiko Yamazaki Professor and Senior Fellow at the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI (HAI) and Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab, also a SIEPR Senior Fellow and NBER Research Associate. He is one of the most cited economists on how digital technology and AI reshape productivity, work, and measured economic value.

### Their future thesis (one line)
AI is a general-purpose technology that can drive a productivity boom and broadly shared prosperity, but only if we steer it to augment human capability rather than fall into the "Turing Trap" of merely imitating and replacing people.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Turing Trap: The Promise and Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence" (Spring 2022), https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/the-turing-trap-the-promise-peril-of-human-like-artificial-intelligence/
  Argues that building AI to mimic humans concentrates wealth and power and depresses wages, while building AI to extend what humans can do raises productivity and bargaining power. The choice between substitution and augmentation is a design and policy decision, not a technological inevitability, and it shapes who captures the gains.

- "Generative AI at Work" (April 2023, published Quarterly Journal of Economics May 2025), https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
  A field study of customer-support agents finds that access to a generative AI assistant raised productivity by roughly 14 percent on average, with the largest gains going to less-experienced and lower-skilled workers. This suggests AI can compress skill gaps and diffuse expertise, pointing to a more equalizing path if deployed to support frontline workers.

- "The Economics of Transformative AI" (2025), https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/economics-transformative-ai
  A University of Chicago Press volume he edited with Ajay Agrawal and Anton Korinek that maps the research frontier on what advanced AI means for growth, labor, inequality, and market structure. It frames transformative AI as a serious near-term scenario economists must model rather than dismiss.

- "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts About the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence" (November 2025), https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf
  Uses payroll data to show early labor-market effects of generative AI concentrated among young workers in the most AI-exposed occupations. It is among the first empirical readings that AI is already bending entry-level hiring, an early signal of the disruption ahead.

- "AI and the Fed" (July 2025), https://www.nber.org/papers/w33998
  With Sophia Kazinnik, examines how AI could change central banking, from data analysis to forecasting and the conduct of monetary policy. It is a concrete look at how transformative AI reshapes a core economic institution, not just private firms.

### Hooks for the brain
- Feeds the "AI as productivity boom vs. mass displacement" debate, with Brynjolfsson on the optimistic-but-conditional side.
- Augmentation vs. automation framing (the Turing Trap) is a core lens for the future-of-work thesis.
- Empirical evidence that AI compresses skill gaps and hits entry-level jobs first informs inequality and labor-market scenarios.
- Pairs directly with Korinek and Autor; the three overlap on the economics of transformative AI.

## 182. David Autor

#source
Domain: Economics of AI; MIT labor economist; Work of the Future
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://economics.mit.edu/people/faculty/david-h-autor (MIT faculty page); profile at https://shapingwork.mit.edu/about-us/david-autor/
- Blog / where published: MIT Shaping the Future of Work / Stone Center (https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/), NBER, plus essays in Noema, NYT Opinion, and journals
- X / social: https://twitter.com/davidautor (X); initiative account https://twitter.com/MITshapingwork
- Podcast / other: Frequent guest (e.g., The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart, EconTalk, MIT and VoxDev interviews)
- Paywall: no (working papers free; some journal and newspaper versions paywalled)

### Who they are
Daniel and Gail Rubinfeld Professor in the MIT Department of Economics, co-director of the NBER Labor Studies Program and faculty co-director of the MIT Stone Center on Inequality and Shaping the Future of Work. Dubbed "the academic voice of the American worker," he is a leading authority on how technology and globalization reshape jobs, wages, and inequality.

### Their future thesis (one line)
AI does not have to hollow out the middle class; if steered toward extending worker expertise rather than replacing it, AI could rebuild middle-skill jobs and raise the value of human judgment, but the outcome depends on choices, not the technology alone.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs" (February 2024), https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140
  Argues that the defining economic question of AI is not mass unemployment but the distribution of work and wages. Autor contends AI could lower the expertise barrier to high-stakes decision work, letting more workers do valuable middle-skill tasks and reversing decades of labor-market polarization, if deployed to complement rather than displace.

- "New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940-2018" (March 2024, Quarterly Journal of Economics), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/new-frontiers-the-origins-and-content-of-new-work-1940-2018/
  Documents that roughly 60 percent of jobs in 2018 were in occupations that did not exist in 1940, showing technology creates as well as destroys work. It also finds the character of new work has shifted from middle-class augmentation toward both high-pay and low-pay extremes, framing why where AI creates jobs matters as much as how many.

- "Building Pro-Worker AI" (February 2026, The Hamilton Project), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/building-pro-worker-ai/
  With Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, lays out concrete policy and design directions to push AI toward augmenting workers, including tax, research-funding, and procurement levers. It is a practical playbook for steering AI's trajectory rather than accepting a displacement-heavy default.

- "What Makes New Work Different from More Work?" (forthcoming March 2026, Annual Review of Economics), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/what-makes-new-work-different-from-more-work/
  Distinguishes technologies that merely expand existing tasks from those that create genuinely new kinds of work, and asks where AI will fall. The answer shapes whether AI raises demand for human labor or simply automates current jobs.

- "The gains will be substantial: the AI shock is looking a lot like the China shock" (Fortune coverage, May 10, 2026), https://fortune.com/2026/05/10/ai-boom-china-shock-job-displacement-apollo-economist-torsten-slok-david-autor/
  Autor reframes the AI labor shock through his China-shock research: disruption is real and concentrated, but the aggregate gains can be large if displaced workers are supported through the transition. The analogy warns that diffuse benefits and concentrated pain require active adjustment policy, a lesson the US largely failed in the China case.

### Hooks for the brain
- Central to the future-of-work thesis: AI as middle-class rebuilder vs. polarizer.
- Augmentation-over-automation framing aligns with Brynjolfsson; both stress design and policy choices.
- His China-shock work supplies the historical template for how a labor shock plays out, linking AI economics to deglobalization themes (overlaps with Zeihan).
- "New work" research grounds optimistic scenarios in evidence that technology historically creates new job categories.

## 183. Anton Korinek

#source
Domain: Economics of AI; UVA; economics of transformative AI
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.korinek.com/
- Blog / where published: Economics of Transformative AI (EconTAI) Initiative (https://www.econtai.org), Brookings (https://www.brookings.edu/people/anton-korinek/), NBER, IMF Finance & Development; research list at https://www.korinek.com/research
- X / social: https://twitter.com/akorinek (X, @akorinek)
- Podcast / other: AI-narrated "deep dive" audio summaries of his papers (linked on his site); frequent conference and central-bank speaker
- Paywall: no (working papers, PDFs, and Brookings/IMF pieces are free; some journal versions paywalled)

### Who they are
Professor in the Department of Economics and Darden School of Business at the University of Virginia and Faculty Director of the Economics of Transformative AI (EconTAI) Initiative, plus nonresident senior fellow at Brookings and the Peterson Institute and a member of Anthropic's Economic Advisory Council. Named to the 2025 TIME100 AI list, he is a leading economist modeling the macroeconomics of a possible transition to artificial general intelligence.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Economists and policymakers should plan now for scenarios in which AI approaches or reaches human-level capability, because transformative AI could drive explosive growth while making human labor economically redundant, demanding new approaches to taxation, distribution, and the social contract.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Scenarios for the Transition to AGI" (March 2024), https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/viob7f5yv13zy0ziezlcg/AGI_Scenarios.pdf?rlkey=8hxq9rm82kksocw1zjilcxf8v&dl=0
  With Donghyun Suh, models how output and wages evolve as AI automates an expanding share of human tasks. The key result: wages can rise for a while but may collapse once AI can do essentially everything humans can, a scenario economists should take seriously rather than rule out.

- "Scenario Planning for an A(G)I Future" (December 2023, IMF Finance & Development), https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/Scenario-Planning-for-an-AGI-future-Anton-korinek
  Makes the case that policymakers must prepare for the possibility of human-level AI even amid deep uncertainty, using scenario planning rather than single point forecasts. It is an accessible statement of why "this might really happen" deserves a place in mainstream economic policy.

- "Economic Growth under Transformative AI" (September 2025), https://www.korinek.com/research
  With Phil Trammell, surveys the wide range of possibilities for growth, wages, and the labor share under transformative AI, from modest acceleration to explosive takeoff. It is a guide to the parameter choices that determine which future we get, mapping the debate's intellectual terrain.

- "When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth?" (January 2026), https://www.econtai.org/research/AutomatingAIResearch.pdf
  With co-authors, examines recursive self-improvement, where AI accelerates AI research itself, and finds the threshold for a growth take-off may be within reach. This connects the abstract idea of an intelligence explosion to concrete economic growth dynamics.

- "Public Finance in the Age of AI: A Primer" (January 2026, Brookings / NBER volume), https://www.brookings.edu/articles/public-finance-age-ai-primer/
  With Lee Lockwood, asks what governments should tax once AGI erodes labor income and consumption, the traditional tax bases. It is an early, practical treatment of how to fund the state and redistribute in a post-labor economy.

### Hooks for the brain
- Anchor for the "transformative AI / AGI" scenario in the brain: takes explosive growth and labor redundancy seriously.
- Public-finance and redistribution work feeds questions about UBI, taxation, and the social contract in a post-work world.
- Co-editor with Brynjolfsson and Agrawal on the transformative-AI research agenda; central node connecting the AI economists.
- Recursive self-improvement and intelligence-explosion economics inform the fastest, most disruptive timelines.

## 184. Daniel Susskind

#source
Domain: Economics of AI; King's College London; future of work
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.danielsusskind.com/
- Blog / where published: Writing page at https://www.danielsusskind.com/writing/; essays in the FT, The Times, and other outlets; lectures via Gresham College
- X / social: https://www.linkedin.com/in/daniel-susskind-68244829 (LinkedIn); active on X historically (@danielsusskind)
- Podcast / other: TED Talk on the future of work (1.7M+ views); frequent podcast and lecture guest; Gresham College public lectures (free video)
- Paywall: partial (his site, lectures, and TED talk are free; books are paid and some op-eds sit behind paywalls)

### Who they are
Writer and economist; Mercers' School Memorial Professor of Business at Gresham College, Research Professor at King's College London, Digital Fellow at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab, and Senior Research Associate at Oxford's Institute for Ethics in AI. A member of the UK Government's Expert Panel on AI and the Future of Work, he is one of the most widely read public thinkers on technology and the future of jobs.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Over the long run, advancing AI will steadily encroach on tasks once thought uniquely human, so societies must prepare for a world with far less paid work, rethinking how people earn income, find meaning, and distribute the wealth that machines create.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "A World Without Work" (2020), https://www.danielsusskind.com/books/
  Argues that technological unemployment, long dismissed by economists, is becoming a real long-term prospect as machines take on ever more tasks. The book reframes the central challenges of a low-work future as three problems: inequality (how to share prosperity), political power (concentrated in those who own the technology), and meaning (purpose beyond a job).

- "Growth: A Reckoning" (2024), https://www.danielsusskind.com/growth-a-reckoning
  Examines how two centuries of economic growth lifted billions out of poverty but also drove inequality, instability, and environmental harm, and argues we must redirect growth rather than abandon it. An Obama "Favourite Books of 2024" pick and FT Business Book of the Year runner-up, it reframes what progress should mean as AI accelerates output.

- "What Should My Children Do? How to Flourish in the Age of AI" (forthcoming 2026), https://www.danielsusskind.com/books/
  A forthcoming book turning his future-of-work analysis toward education and human development: what skills, dispositions, and choices help people thrive when AI is pervasive. It signals his shift from diagnosing disruption to advising how individuals and families should adapt.

- "The Future of the Professions" (2015, with Richard Susskind), https://www.danielsusskind.com/books/
  Argues that AI and automation will dismantle the traditional gatekeeping model of doctors, lawyers, teachers, and other professionals, redistributing their expertise more widely. An early and influential map of how knowledge work itself gets transformed, prefiguring today's generative-AI debates.

- TED Talk: "3 myths about the future of work (and why they're not true)" (2017/2020), https://www.danielsusskind.com/video
  A widely viewed talk arguing that past reassurances about technology always creating enough new jobs may not hold this time. It is his most accessible statement of why the future-of-work problem could be different in degree and kind.

### Hooks for the brain
- Anchors the more pessimistic future-of-work scenario: a long-run world with structurally less paid work.
- His three problems (inequality, power, meaning) provide a framework for the social-contract and post-work strand of the brain.
- "Growth: A Reckoning" links AI and automation to the broader debate over what economic progress should optimize for.
- Bridges economics of AI with ethics and education (Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI), useful for human-flourishing questions.

## 185. Peter Zeihan

#source
Domain: Geopolitics and demographics; geopolitical analyst; deglobalization and demographics
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://zeihan.com/
- Blog / where published: "Zeihan on Geopolitics" newsletter and blog (free) at https://zeihan.com/newsletter/; books published via Harper Business / Twelve
- X / social: X (@PeterZeihan) and a large YouTube channel "Zeihan on Geopolitics" (https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCsy9I56PY3IngCf_VGjunMQ)
- Podcast / other: Near-daily short YouTube video analyses; frequent guest on business and finance podcasts (e.g., ARK Invest FYI, Technovation)
- Paywall: no (newsletter, blog, and videos are free; books and custom client work are paid)

### Who they are
Geopolitical strategist and founder of the consulting firm Zeihan on Geopolitics (founded 2012), previously a vice president at the intelligence firm Stratfor. He analyzes the world through the lens of geography, demographics, and energy, and is a New York Times bestselling author known for blunt, contrarian forecasts.

### Their future thesis (one line)
The US-secured era of globalization is ending, and as American security guarantees recede, an aging and shrinking global population plus broken supply chains will trigger deglobalization, regional fragmentation, and the relative decline of export- and import-dependent powers, especially China.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "The End of the World Is Just the Beginning: Mapping the Collapse of Globalization" (2022), https://zeihan.com/the-end-of-the-world-is-just-the-beginning/
  Argues that globalization was an artificial, US-underwritten order that is now unwinding, and that the next decades bring deglobalization, broken supply chains, and falling living standards in countries dependent on trade. Pairs this with a demographic collapse argument: much of the world is aging past the point of recovery, gutting consumption and labor forces.

- "The Accidental Superpower: Ten Years On" (2024 updated edition; original 2014), https://zeihan.com/tas/
  Lays out his geographic case that the US, with navigable rivers, arable land, ocean buffers, and favorable demographics, is uniquely positioned to thrive even as the global order frays. The tenth-anniversary edition revisits his original predictions about American advantage and global disorder.

- "Disunited Nations: The Scramble for Power in an Ungoverned World" (2020), https://zeihan.com/disunited-nations/
  Forecasts a world without a global policeman, where regional powers compete and former great powers (China, Germany, Russia, Saudi Arabia) face structural decline. It maps which countries are positioned to win or lose as the international system fragments.

- "Zeihan on Geopolitics" newsletter and daily video analysis (ongoing), https://zeihan.com/newsletter/
  His running commentary applies the geography-demographics-energy framework to live events such as the Ukraine war, shipping disruptions, and energy markets. This is where he updates and stress-tests his deglobalization thesis in near real time, including his framing of the mid-2020s as the final phase of globalization.

### Hooks for the brain
- Primary voice for the deglobalization and supply-chain-fragmentation scenario.
- Demographic-collapse thesis (aging populations, shrinking workforces) is a core input to the demographics strand; overlaps with Sciubba though more alarmist.
- Bull case for the US and bear case for China feed great-power and economic-geography forecasts (overlaps with Bremmer, though Zeihan is more deterministic).
- Caveat for the brain: his forecasts are bold and contested; treat as a strong-thesis provocation, balance against more measured analysts.

## 186. Ian Bremmer

#source
Domain: Geopolitics and demographics; President, Eurasia Group and GZERO Media; Top Risks
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.gzeromedia.com/ (media) and https://www.eurasiagroup.net/people/ibremmer (firm bio)
- Blog / where published: GZERO Media columns and newsletters; Eurasia Group "Top Risks" annual report (https://www.eurasiagroup.net/issues/top-risks-2026)
- X / social: X (@ianbremmer); also active on LinkedIn, Threads, and Substack ("GZERO" / Ian Bremmer)
- Podcast / other: "GZERO World with Ian Bremmer" weekly podcast and US public-television show; "Quick Take" video commentary
- Paywall: no (GZERO content, newsletters, and the Top Risks headline report are free; bespoke Eurasia Group consulting is paid)

### Who they are
Political scientist and entrepreneur; founder and president of Eurasia Group, the political-risk consultancy, and of GZERO Media. He popularized the "G-Zero" idea of a leaderless world and is among the most visible public interpreters of global political risk, best known for the firm's annual "Top Risks" forecast.

### Their future thesis (one line)
We live in a "G-Zero" world with no single power or coalition willing to lead, and the defining risk of the coming decade is that this leadership vacuum, combined with a fracturing US-led order and the disruptive force of AI, drives instability that existing institutions are unprepared to manage.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "Eurasia Group Top Risks 2026" (January 5, 2026), https://www.eurasiagroup.net/issues/top-risks-2026
  The firm's flagship annual forecast names the "US political revolution" as the top risk, arguing the US is dismantling the very global order it built. Other listed risks include China's position in the global energy transition, US intervention in the Western Hemisphere, a weakening European center, and pressure on AI business models, a concrete map of the year's geopolitical fault lines.

- "Ian Bremmer: US ending its own global order" (Axios interview, January 5, 2026), https://www.axios.com/2026/01/05/ian-bremmer-us-ending-own-global-order
  Summarizes his central 2026 argument that the biggest geopolitical shock is internal to the US, as its government steps back from the postwar liberal order it long underwrote. It frames the decade ahead as defined by American retrenchment and the resulting global power vacuum.

- "The Power of Crisis: How Three Threats and Our Response Will Change the World" (2022), https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Power-of-Crisis/Ian-Bremmer/9781982167516
  Argues that three accelerating crises, pandemics, climate change, and disruptive technologies including AI, are global by nature and cannot be solved by any country alone. It makes the case that shared threats could force cooperation and new institutions, an optimistic counterpoint to his G-Zero diagnosis.

- "GZERO World with Ian Bremmer" (ongoing weekly show and podcast), https://www.gzeromedia.com/gzero-world
  His running interview and analysis program where he interprets live geopolitics, from US-China relations to AI governance and Middle East and Ukraine conflicts. This is the best place to track how his risk framework evolves week to week.

- "Eurasia Group Top Risks 2025: The G-Zero Wins" (January 2025), https://www.eurasiagroup.net/issues/top-risks-2025
  The prior year's forecast argued the leaderless G-Zero order had effectively triumphed, with no actor able or willing to set global rules. Useful as the immediate precursor that sets up the 2026 "US political revolution" thesis.

### Hooks for the brain
- Primary source for the "G-Zero / leaderless world" framing of geopolitical futures.
- "Top Risks" reports are a yearly structured snapshot of near-term geopolitical scenarios, ideal anchor data for the brain.
- His treatment of AI as a geopolitical and governance disruptor links the AI-economics strand to the geopolitics strand.
- More institution- and cooperation-oriented than Zeihan; useful to balance Zeihan's deterministic deglobalization with a risk-and-response lens.

## 187. Jennifer Sciubba

#source
Domain: Geopolitics and demographics; President, Population Reference Bureau; political demographer
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.prb.org/people/jennifer-dabbs-sciubba/ (PRB profile); PRB org site https://www.prb.org/
- Blog / where published: Population Reference Bureau (https://www.prb.org/); essays in The Atlantic, Harvard Business Review, The Washington Post, The Hill, and The New York Times
- X / social: Active on X and LinkedIn (links via her PRB profile); PRB on X @PRBdata
- Podcast / other: TED Talk "The Truth About Human Population Decline" (2023, 1.2M+ views); guest on The Ezra Klein Show, NPR, C-SPAN Book TV
- Paywall: partial (PRB research and her TED talk are free; some newspaper op-eds and her book are paid)

### Who they are
President and CEO of the Population Reference Bureau (since May 2024) and an internationally recognized expert in political demography, formerly a tenured professor at Rhodes College and a demographics consultant to the US Office of the Secretary of Defense. She specializes in translating fertility, mortality, and migration data into actionable insight for policymakers and the public.

### Their future thesis (one line)
The twenty-first century is defined not by runaway population growth but by differential growth: a widening split between aging, shrinking rich countries and young, fast-growing poor ones, and how societies respond to that divergence will shape security, economies, and well-being.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "8 Billion and Counting: How Sex, Death, and Migration Shape Our World" (W.W. Norton, 2022), https://www.amazon.com/Billion-Counting-Death-Migration-Shape/dp/1324002700
  Argues that the dominant demographic story is divergence, not a single global trend: some countries face rapid aging and decline while others are still young and growing fast. She makes the case that fertility, mortality, and migration patterns today point to the investments needed now, and warns against panic framings of both "overpopulation" and "population collapse."

- TED Talk: "The Truth About Human Population Decline" (2023), https://www.ted.com/talks/jennifer_d_sciubba_the_truth_about_human_population_decline
  Pushes back on alarmist narratives about falling birth rates, arguing that decline is neither uniform nor necessarily catastrophic and that policy responses matter more than the raw numbers. Her most-viewed public statement of why demographic destiny is more nuanced than headlines suggest.

- "The Mistaken Assumption That Immigration Is Inevitable" (The Atlantic, March 31, 2022), https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2022/03/global-migration-population-growth-myth/629387/
  Challenges the assumption that aging rich countries can always rely on a steady inflow of migrants to offset shrinking workforces. As source countries themselves age and develop, she argues, the future supply of migrants is far less guaranteed than policymakers assume.

- "Birthrates Are Plummeting Around the World. Why?" (The Ezra Klein Show, NYT, March 19, 2024), https://www.nytimes.com/2024/03/19/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-jennifer-sciubba.html
  A long-form conversation unpacking the global fertility decline, its uneven distribution, and what it means for economies, pensions, and geopolitics. A clear primer on why low birth rates are reshaping the long-term outlook for both rich and developing nations.

- "Get to Know PRB CEO Jennifer D. Sciubba" (PRB, 2024), https://www.prb.org/articles/get-to-know-prb-ceo-jennifer-d-sciubba/
  PRB's introduction to her leadership and approach, emphasizing data-driven, forward-looking demography to inform policy on urgent global challenges. Useful context on how she intends to steer PRB's population-trend work over the coming years.

### Hooks for the brain
- Lead voice for the demographics strand: aging, fertility decline, and migration over a 10-to-20-year horizon.
- "Differential growth" framing is a key corrective to single-trend population narratives; balances alarmist "collapse" takes.
- Her skepticism that migration will automatically offset aging connects demographics to labor-supply and economic scenarios.
- Complements and tempers Zeihan's demographic-collapse thesis with a more measured, data-first read; pairs with Bremmer on geopolitics-of-population.

## 188. Tom Davidson

#source
Domain: AI governance and forecasting
Verified: June 2026

### Read them
- Homepage: https://www.forethought.org/people/tom-davidson
- Blog / where published / org: Forethought (https://www.forethought.org/research); earlier work via Open Philanthropy
- X / social: https://x.com/TomDavidsonX
- Podcast / other: Personal site https://tomdavidson-ai.github.io/ ; LessWrong https://www.lesswrong.com/users/tom-davidson-1 ; guest on 80,000 Hours and AXRP
- Paywall: no

### Who they are
Senior Research Fellow at Forethought (AI strategy non-profit), and the leading author behind Open Philanthropy's compute-centric reports on AI timelines and explosive growth. Previously a research scientist at the UK AI Security Institute and a Senior Research Fellow at Open Philanthropy.

### Their future thesis (one line)
Once AI can automate AI research, progress could compound into a fast software intelligence explosion within a few years, and that speed creates a distinct new danger: a tiny group seizing power through AI loyal only to them.

### Key future-relevant pieces
- "AI-Enabled Coups: How a Small Group Could Use AI to Seize Power" (15 April 2025), https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-enabled-coups-how-a-small-group-could-use-ai-to-seize-power
  Argues that broadly capable AI introduces a new political risk: a single person or small clique could stage a coup, even inside an established democracy. The danger comes from AI systems built to be secretly loyal to specific leaders, from replacing human soldiers and officials with such systems, and from a small group gaining exclusive access to superhuman capabilities in weapons, strategy, and persuasion.

- "How quick and big would a software intelligence explosion be?" (4 August 2025), https://www.forethought.org/research/how-quick-and-big-would-a-software-intelligence-explosion-be
  Models the dynamics of an intelligence explosion driven by automated AI research, where AI improves AI software faster than humans could. It examines how steep and how large the resulting jump in capability could be before bottlenecks bite. The piece is part of his broader compute-centric framework for forecasting takeoff speeds.

- "The Industrial Explosion" (21 May 2025), with Rose Hadshar, https://www.forethought.org/research/the-industrial-explosion
  Extends the takeoff analysis from software into the physical economy, asking how fast AI plus robotics could expand manufacturing and infrastructure. It frames a possible period of explosive industrial growth following AI-driven research acceleration. This connects cognitive takeoff to real-world transformation of production.
