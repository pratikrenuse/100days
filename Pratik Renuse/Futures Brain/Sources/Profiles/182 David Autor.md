## Who they are
Daniel and Gail Rubinfeld Professor in the MIT Department of Economics, co-director of the NBER Labor Studies Program and faculty co-director of the MIT Stone Center on Inequality and Shaping the Future of Work. Dubbed "the academic voice of the American worker," he is a leading authority on how technology and globalization reshape jobs, wages, and inequality.

## Their future thesis (one line)
AI does not have to hollow out the middle class; if steered toward extending worker expertise rather than replacing it, AI could rebuild middle-skill jobs and raise the value of human judgment, but the outcome depends on choices, not the technology alone.

## Key future-relevant pieces
- "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs" (February 2024), https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140
  Argues that the defining economic question of AI is not mass unemployment but the distribution of work and wages. Autor contends AI could lower the expertise barrier to high-stakes decision work, letting more workers do valuable middle-skill tasks and reversing decades of labor-market polarization, if deployed to complement rather than displace.

- "New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940-2018" (March 2024, Quarterly Journal of Economics), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/new-frontiers-the-origins-and-content-of-new-work-1940-2018/
  Documents that roughly 60 percent of jobs in 2018 were in occupations that did not exist in 1940, showing technology creates as well as destroys work. It also finds the character of new work has shifted from middle-class augmentation toward both high-pay and low-pay extremes, framing why where AI creates jobs matters as much as how many.

- "Building Pro-Worker AI" (February 2026, The Hamilton Project), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/building-pro-worker-ai/
  With Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, lays out concrete policy and design directions to push AI toward augmenting workers, including tax, research-funding, and procurement levers. It is a practical playbook for steering AI's trajectory rather than accepting a displacement-heavy default.

- "What Makes New Work Different from More Work?" (forthcoming March 2026, Annual Review of Economics), https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/what-makes-new-work-different-from-more-work/
  Distinguishes technologies that merely expand existing tasks from those that create genuinely new kinds of work, and asks where AI will fall. The answer shapes whether AI raises demand for human labor or simply automates current jobs.

- "The gains will be substantial: the AI shock is looking a lot like the China shock" (Fortune coverage, May 10, 2026), https://fortune.com/2026/05/10/ai-boom-china-shock-job-displacement-apollo-economist-torsten-slok-david-autor/
  Autor reframes the AI labor shock through his China-shock research: disruption is real and concentrated, but the aggregate gains can be large if displaced workers are supported through the transition. The analogy warns that diffuse benefits and concentrated pain require active adjustment policy, a lesson the US largely failed in the China case.

## Hooks for the brain
- Central to the future-of-work thesis: AI as middle-class rebuilder vs. polarizer.
- Augmentation-over-automation framing aligns with Brynjolfsson; both stress design and policy choices.
- His China-shock work supplies the historical template for how a labor shock plays out, linking AI economics to deglobalization themes (overlaps with Zeihan).
- "New work" research grounds optimistic scenarios in evidence that technology historically creates new job categories.
