## Who they are
Jerry Yang and Akiko Yamazaki Professor and Senior Fellow at the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI (HAI) and Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab, also a SIEPR Senior Fellow and NBER Research Associate. He is one of the most cited economists on how digital technology and AI reshape productivity, work, and measured economic value.

## Their future thesis (one line)
AI is a general-purpose technology that can drive a productivity boom and broadly shared prosperity, but only if we steer it to augment human capability rather than fall into the "Turing Trap" of merely imitating and replacing people.

## Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Turing Trap: The Promise and Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence" (Spring 2022), https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/the-turing-trap-the-promise-peril-of-human-like-artificial-intelligence/
  Argues that building AI to mimic humans concentrates wealth and power and depresses wages, while building AI to extend what humans can do raises productivity and bargaining power. The choice between substitution and augmentation is a design and policy decision, not a technological inevitability, and it shapes who captures the gains.

- "Generative AI at Work" (April 2023, published Quarterly Journal of Economics May 2025), https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
  A field study of customer-support agents finds that access to a generative AI assistant raised productivity by roughly 14 percent on average, with the largest gains going to less-experienced and lower-skilled workers. This suggests AI can compress skill gaps and diffuse expertise, pointing to a more equalizing path if deployed to support frontline workers.

- "The Economics of Transformative AI" (2025), https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/economics-transformative-ai
  A University of Chicago Press volume he edited with Ajay Agrawal and Anton Korinek that maps the research frontier on what advanced AI means for growth, labor, inequality, and market structure. It frames transformative AI as a serious near-term scenario economists must model rather than dismiss.

- "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts About the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence" (November 2025), https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf
  Uses payroll data to show early labor-market effects of generative AI concentrated among young workers in the most AI-exposed occupations. It is among the first empirical readings that AI is already bending entry-level hiring, an early signal of the disruption ahead.

- "AI and the Fed" (July 2025), https://www.nber.org/papers/w33998
  With Sophia Kazinnik, examines how AI could change central banking, from data analysis to forecasting and the conduct of monetary policy. It is a concrete look at how transformative AI reshapes a core economic institution, not just private firms.

## Hooks for the brain
- Feeds the "AI as productivity boom vs. mass displacement" debate, with Brynjolfsson on the optimistic-but-conditional side.
- Augmentation vs. automation framing (the Turing Trap) is a core lens for the future-of-work thesis.
- Empirical evidence that AI compresses skill gaps and hits entry-level jobs first informs inequality and labor-market scenarios.
- Pairs directly with Korinek and Autor; the three overlap on the economics of transformative AI.
