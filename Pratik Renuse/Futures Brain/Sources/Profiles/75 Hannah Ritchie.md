## Who they are
Data scientist and researcher. Head of Research at Our World in Data and a Senior Researcher at the University of Oxford. She specializes in turning large datasets on energy, climate, food, and global development into clear, myth-busting charts and explainers for non-experts. Author of "Not the End of the World," a data-driven case for cautious environmental optimism.

## Their future thesis (one line)
We are the first generation that can plausibly build a sustainable world, and the data shows real, fast progress (especially in clean energy and electrification), so the right posture is urgent, evidence-based optimism rather than doom or complacency.

## Key future-relevant pieces
- "Will Africa still be energy-poor in 2050?" (17 Mar 2026), https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/africa-energy-poverty-projections
  Uses long-range projections to warn that on current trajectories, electricity consumption across much of Africa will remain extremely low for decades. A sobering counter to blanket optimism: the transition is uneven, and energy poverty is the development challenge that cheap solar has not yet solved.

- "How much electricity does AI consume? [2025 summary]" (5 May 2026), https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/ai-electricity-2025
  A data-grounded reckoning with the AI-energy panic. She quantifies the share of electricity used by data centers and the real footprint of chatbots, cutting through hype in both directions. Important for any 10-to-20-year view of how AI demand reshapes the grid.

- "How quickly do electric car sales translate into cars on the road?" (12 Jun 2026), https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/ev-fleet-transition
  Explores the lag between EV sales share and EV fleet share, a crucial and underappreciated dynamic. Sales can hit majority well before the cars on the road do, which reframes how fast transport emissions actually fall.

- "Not the End of the World" (2024), https://hannahritchie.com/not-the-end-of-the-world/
  Her flagship book and clearest thesis statement. It walks through seven big environmental problems (climate, air pollution, deforestation, food, biodiversity, ocean plastics, overfishing) and argues each is solvable with current trajectories and tools, making the case for "the first generation that can build a sustainable planet."
