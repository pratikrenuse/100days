## Who they are
Biochemist who won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for computational protein design, and Director of the Institute for Protein Design at the University of Washington. He pioneered tools like RoseTTAFold and RFdiffusion that let scientists design entirely new proteins, and has co-founded more than 20 biotech companies.

## Their future thesis (one line)
AI-driven de novo protein design is becoming a general-purpose technology for building made-to-order medicines, vaccines, antibodies, enzymes, and materials, shifting biology from discovering natural proteins to engineering new ones on demand.

## Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Promise of Protein Design: A Q&A with Nobel Laureate David Baker" (GEN Biotechnology, 17 February 2025), https://www.liebertpub.com/doi/10.1089/genbio.2025.0004
  In this Q&A, Baker explains how AI methods now let researchers design protein binders directly from a target's sequence, without needing its solved structure. He discusses applications spanning vaccines, therapeutics, and company formation, and reflects on mentorship and translation. It is a clear statement of where he sees protein design heading.

- "Teaching AI to build antibodies from scratch" / "Atomically accurate de novo design of antibodies with RFdiffusion" (Institute for Protein Design, Nature, November 2025), https://www.ipd.uw.edu/2025/11/rfantibody-in-nature/
  Baker's team published a method to design antibodies de novo that bind user-specified targets with atomic-level precision, with the RFantibody software released freely. Baker argues AI antibody design will transform biotech and pharma by enabling precise targeting and simpler drug development. This is a direct line into the roughly $100 billion antibody drug market.

- "A diffusion model for protein design" / RFdiffusion (Institute for Protein Design, 2022), https://www.ipd.uw.edu/2022/12/a-diffusion-model-for-protein-design/
  Introduces RFdiffusion, the generative model that lets scientists design novel proteins not found in nature for applications including pharmaceuticals, vaccines, nanomaterials, and biosensors. It marked a step change from designing variants toward generating proteins from scratch. The tool underpins much of the lab's later work.

- "Seven technologies to watch in 2024: protein design" (Nature, featured), https://www.ipd.uw.edu/2024/01/nature-seven-technologies-to-watch-in-2024/
  Nature highlighted de novo protein design, much of it from Baker's lab, as a technology maturing into a practical tool for custom enzymes, biomaterials, and therapeutics. The piece frames protein design as moving from research curiosity to broad utility. It signals mainstream scientific recognition of the field's trajectory.
