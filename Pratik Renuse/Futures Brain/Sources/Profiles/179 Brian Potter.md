## Who they are
Structural engineer and former Katerra engineering manager who writes Construction Physics, a widely read newsletter (over 74,000 subscribers) on the technology and economics of building, infrastructure, and industrial production. He is a senior fellow at the Institute for Progress and author of "The Origins of Efficiency."

## Their future thesis (one line)
Productivity gains come from understanding the deep mechanics of production processes, not from silver-bullet innovations; most "new" ideas in construction and manufacturing have long histories, and real progress depends on learning curves, volume, and unglamorous process improvement.

## Key future-relevant pieces
- "The Origins of Efficiency" (Stripe Press, Oct 2025), https://press.stripe.com/origins-of-efficiency
  Potter's book arguing that improving production efficiency, making things faster, cheaper, and more reliably, is the engine behind major historical change. It dissects production processes across steel, semiconductors, wind turbines, shipping, and cars, with cases like penicillin scale-up and the Ford Model T. It is his fullest statement of how industrial progress actually happens.

- "50 Things I've Learned Writing Construction Physics" (Apr 17, 2025), https://www.construction-physics.com/p/50-things-ive-learned-writing-construction
  A distilled set of takeaways from nearly 200 essays, including that construction tasks have not gotten cheaper since the 1950s, prefab is usually not cheaper than on-site building, and learning curves drive most cost declines but eventually stall. It is the single best on-ramp to his worldview. The recurring lesson: intuitive explanations are usually wrong and contingency matters more than inevitability.

- "Will We Ever Get Fusion Power?" (Construction Physics), https://www.construction-physics.com/p/will-we-ever-get-fusion-power
  Applies his learning-curve and production-volume lens to fusion, arguing that early-stage technologies often improve far faster than Moore's Law and that cost depends on cumulative production. It is a useful cross-check on the optimism of fusion CEOs. Shows how he reasons about whether a frontier technology will actually scale.

- "Where Are My Damn Learning Curves?" (Construction Physics), https://www.construction-physics.com/p/where-are-my-damn-learning-curves
  Explores why learning curves (costs falling with cumulative production) drive affordability but can stall when improvements get hard to find, contrasting titanium with solar PV despite similar learning rates. It is central to his framework for predicting which technologies get cheap. Helps separate hype from durable cost decline.
