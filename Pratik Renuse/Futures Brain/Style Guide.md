# Style Guide (locked)

The voice and rules every post must clear. Built and tested across the June 2026 session, sharpened on 2026-06-30 into the punchier deepdive format. Inspired by Jayant Mundra's LinkedIn deep dives, in our own words.

Start with [[00 The Master Template]]. It is the single source of truth: the full pattern, the beat-by-beat skeleton, and the **two gold-standard posts it was built from, GE Vernova (power) and Astera Labs (bandwidth)**, both embedded in full. Those two share one identical eight-beat pattern in completely different words: that is the standard now. This guide and [[Post Format (LinkedIn Deepdive)]] hold the same rules in more detail, but note both were written for the older, longer deepdive cut. Where they conflict with the Master Template (the newer, tighter, warmer, jargon-free voice aimed at a normal reader, ~2,000 to 2,200 characters), the Master Template wins.

## The voice

Write like a real person who went down a rabbit hole and cannot wait to tell a smart friend what they found. Curious, informal, genuinely startled. Fast. Confident, but honest about what would prove the take wrong.

Open on a real reaction in your own words, paired with a hard, surprising, dated fact. A punchy conversational hook is wanted, but it has to be fresh. Do not reach for a stock exclamation you have used before, and do not borrow one from an example post. The surprise has to be real, never performed. Then get straight into the story.

What kills the "AI smell":
- Ground everything in concrete, slightly obscure specifics (the real company, the real number with its date, the actual mechanism). Specificity is what makes it human.
- Avoid tidy metaphors stacked on tidy metaphors, and avoid fortune-cookie aphorisms in every paragraph. One earned line at the end is plenty.
- Vary sentence length. Let some sentences just be ordinary.
- Drop announced transitions like "here's the part worth sitting with" if they start to feel like scaffolding.

## Hard rules

- **No em dashes. Ever.** Use commas, colons, periods, or parentheses.
- **Flow, don't announce.** Do not open every `..` section with a stock announcement ("here's the thing", "that's when it hit me", "here's where it gets strange", "the thought I can't shake", "now here's the wild part", "and honestly"). One such signal per post at most. Most sections should open on the substance and connect to the line before, as a consequence ("So..."), a contrast ("But..."), or a continuation, so the post reads as one flowing article, not a stack of announcements. A reaction inside a beat ("Cables!") is fine; an announcement that opens a beat is the problem.
- Genuine conversational shock is fine ("My god", "Wild", "this broke my brain"). Marketing-hype adjectives are not ("stunning", "mind-blowing", "game-changer", "revolutionary", "worship"). The test: a startled friend would say it, an ad would not.
- No emojis.
- Every claim is dated and sourced in the relevant Player or Thesis note, so facts age honestly.
- The hook is the **hidden player** or the unseen cause, not the theme. People know "memory matters". Almost nobody knows SK Hynix. Lead with the unknown name.
- Every post must end on a concrete 10 and 20 year takeaway tied to a pattern. That payoff is the whole point of the post, never optional, never a year-stamped prediction.
- The takeaway must be a specific claim, not a mood. Name what gets bought, what becomes scarce, who collects the money, or a number that crosses a threshold. "We stop buying compute and start buying bandwidth" is a claim. "Funny where the money hides" is filler.
- Teach one real mechanism with concrete, dated numbers. If the post does not leave the reader knowing *how* something works that they did not know before, it is not finished.
- Invent your own connective tissue. Never reuse the signature transitions of an example post ("First up", "delicious irony", "here is the part I keep thinking about", "this is what X actually does"). If a line could be pasted into another post unchanged, it is too generic. (A short "No?" close is sanctioned, Jayant-style, but do not end every single post identically.)
- Kill crutch phrases. If "bottleneck", "one floor below", "the rung", or any pet phrase recurs across posts, find a fresh, specific way to say the real thing.
- Never command the reader or write stage directions. Banned, and anything like them: "Start here", "Think about this", "Think about what that means", "Picture this", "Imagine", "Consider", "Let me explain", "Stay with me", "Now watch". Do not tell the reader to do anything or narrate how to read. Just say it straight and let the facts pull them. First-person reactions ("the part that got me", "my jaw dropped") are fine; reader-commands are not.
- Sanity-check every sentence. No absurd hyperbole or outlier claims a smart reader would call out as nonsense ("raise a hundred billion dollars in a weekend"). If a line is not literally defensible, rewrite it true: "AI companies are raising tens of billions at a time." Exaggeration that breaks under one second of thought kills trust in everything else.

## What kills a post dead (never write like this)
We studied a real post that got zero engagement, zero likes, to lock in the failure mode. Never do any of these. If a draft has any of it, cut it entirely.

- **It's about you, not about what the reader learns.** The dead post was a feelings-journey about the writer's own integrity ("I pride myself...", "the most uncomfortable deliverable of my career", "the most accurate thing I've produced in years"). The reader finished having learned nothing they could repeat. Our "I" is only a guide pointing at an external discovery, never the subject. Using "I" and "you" is good; making the post about you is fatal.
- **Abstraction with no specifics.** "a massive IT firm", "the entire tech sector", "the illusion of control". No names, no real facts, nothing to picture. Always name the concrete company or thing and teach dated facts.
- **Humble-bragging and performed thoughtfulness.** The "look how honest and brave I am" arc reads as performance and gets scrolled past. Deliver the goods, do not preen.
- **A vague big-concept hook.** "Every 5-year plan today is guesswork. Welcome to the illusion of control." That is a thesis statement, not a scroll-stopper. Open on one concrete, surprising fact.
- **Ending on helplessness or a non-conclusion.** The dead post's whole point was "this is unknowable, so I wrote a disclaimer." That deflates the reader. We end on a confident, concrete forecast that leaves them feeling smarter and ahead.
- **Engagement-bait aimed at a narrow clique.** "Modelers, analysts, strategy folks, what do you actually do?" is homework for a tiny group. If we ask anything, it is one easy, universal question anyone can answer.
- **Jargon with no payoff** (DCF, mean-revert, back-tested, margin of safety) that teaches a normal reader nothing.

The single test: if the reader cannot repeat one concrete, surprising thing they just learned, the post is dead. Cut it and start again from a real fact.

## The standing standard (write every post this way)
The canonical template is [[00 The Master Template]], built from **two gold-standard posts**: `Posts/Format Examples/GE Vernova (creator standard).md` and `Posts/Format Examples/Astera Labs (creator standard).md`. Both are reference-grade, study both. They run the same eight beats (personal hook, reveal + reaction, the simple explanation, the hidden player once, the data with a kicker, the realization, a concrete forecast, the today/tomorrow shift close) in different words. Every post follows the pattern:
- **Fixed beats, fresh words.** The structure repeats; the exact phrases must not. Reword the personal investigation beat ("I had to know how that's even possible", "I didn't believe it at first"), the realization beat ("that's when it clicked", "that's when it hit me"), the wrap-up beat ("so here's where I land", "so here's the bet I'd make"), and especially the close, every single time. The two templates use different words for the same beats on purpose: copy the skeleton, not the sentences. The close must vary and must NEVER default to "No?" on every post; a declarative landing or a fresh question both work.
- **Vary the vocabulary too, inside a post and across posts.** Do not lean on one word. The Astera post must not say "wiring" six times; reach for the synonyms (cables, links, connections, switches, the parts in between). Across posts, change the texture words so no two read like the same machine wrote them. Same voice, different words.
- **Jayant layout:** heavy white space, one or two sentence lines, "-" bullets, ".." section breaks, short curiosity transitions.
- **Slippery slide:** every line earns the next.
- **Human and personal:** "I" and "you" throughout, with genuine curiosity and reaction ("I went down a rabbit hole on why, and it changed how I think about the AI race"). Do NOT manufacture a personal credential or a fake "I assumed X, I was wrong" humility beat, and never bolt on an identity claim the reader does not need ("I build software for a living, so..."). Those read as performance and kill the post. Personal means genuine, not performed.
- **Investor-grade numbers:** weave in the figures investors, VCs and analysts care about, capex, growth %, valuations, multiples, distributed with white space, never dumped. Examples: "$725 billion of AI capex this year, up 77%"; "GE Vernova spun out at $115, now near $1,100, nearly 10x".
- **Say it out loud.** Write it the way you'd explain it to one smart friend across a table ("Today? It's near 1,100" beats "it now trades near 1,100"). If a line isn't how you'd actually say it, rewrite it.
- **Keep it under the cap.** Always check the character count; LinkedIn cuts off at 3,000, and the heavy white space adds up. Aim ~2,400 to 2,800. A tighter ~2,000-character cut also works when you want it punchier: keep the full story spine and compress the forecast section (drop the bullets, run the fusion and space beats as one short paragraph).
- **One connected story.** This is the whole thing. The post must read as a single narrative that builds: a small contradiction or mystery, an investigation, a realization, then what it means. Each beat flows into the next. Weave every number INTO a story beat, never as a standalone stat. The moment it reads like a list of impressive figures, the story is dead.
- **Logical order, each fact once.** Claim (the hook), then why it's true (the mechanism), then the proof (the player, the scarcity, the numbers). Do not state the punchline, then the scarcity, then circle back to explain it. That out-of-order repetition (saying "it's a gas turbine", then "you can't buy a turbine", then "here's why a turbine matters") is the most common way the story stops connecting. Also set up every concept before you lean on it: if the forecast says "copper gives up", the mechanism must have already told the reader the cables are copper. A payoff that references something you never introduced reads as disconnected. Read it back line by line and make sure each one is earned by the one before it.
- **Introduce the hidden player once, in the right place.** Reveal who it is, why it matters, and its numbers in one clean moment, at the point the story needs it. Never name it early as a throwaway, then re-explain it later. That breaks the dots.
- **Land on the core takeaway, not the tangent.** The speculative or futuristic stuff (space, fusion, rockets) is a short detour in the middle, never the closing emphasis. The ending returns to the one point the whole post was about. ("It started with a contradiction. The four biggest tech firms will spend 725 billion on AI this year. And yet you still can't buy a turbine before 2029." is story. A bullet list of capex, valuations, and multiples is not.)
- **One single takeaway, plus a REQUIRED 10 and 20 year forecast.** Every post must spell out where this goes by roughly 2030 and 2040, grounded in dated numbers and interlinking other players from the brain (GE Vernova lands on fusion and space; Astera lands on optics and buying bandwidth). It is never optional; it is how the post earns its weight and its future angle. Keep every claim sanity-checked, no em dashes, no reader-commands.

## The format (LinkedIn deepdive layout)

- Heavy white space, Jayant-style. Almost every line is its own paragraph (one or two sentences, never more), with a blank line between. No dense blocks.
- `..` on its own line marks the bigger turns between sections.
- Use `-` bullets liberally: for the punchy dated facts, and also for a run of short rhetorical questions ("- Why does Goa feel more expensive than Da Nang?").
- Short standalone transition lines that build curiosity ("But this gets wild.", "And that is what flipped it for me.", "Why is everyone suddenly serious?"). These are statements, not reader-commands, so they are fine.
- A strong first line that survives the "see more" fold on its own.
- Rhetorical question-and-answer is the signature move for delivering numbers ("Investment? X. Outcome? Y."). Use it two or three times, not ten.
- Length around 300 to 400 words, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 characters, always comfortably under LinkedIn's 3,000 cap (check the count). Substantial enough to earn dwell time (see [[LinkedIn Creator Playbook]]), but shorter and simpler beats long and dense.

## The shape (not a script)

**One thesis x one hidden player x one 10-to-20-year implication.** Follow the arc of functions in [[Post Format (LinkedIn Deepdive)]], never a fixed set of phrases:

Reorder the reader's world with a surprising dated fact. Set the stakes and name the hidden player. Show the mechanism in concrete numbers, and spend the most words here. Drop a short dated evidence list. Turn on one non-obvious observation specific to this story (the shift). Then add the 10 and 20 year outlook: using our own interlinked notes, sketch where this goes in roughly 10 and 20 years, grounded in real forecasts, with projections clearly labeled as bets. This interlinked outlook is the payoff only our brain can deliver.

Write every transition fresh. Reorder or drop a beat when the story wants it. Two posts should never feel like the same fill-in-the-blanks with the nouns swapped.

## Pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Opens on a genuine reaction plus a surprising dated fact, hidden player not theme
- [ ] Follows the arc (functions, not fixed phrases), with `..` between turns
- [ ] Teaches one real mechanism with hard, dated numbers
- [ ] Every number is in the Player/Thesis note with a date and source
- [ ] Zero em dashes
- [ ] Genuine shock, no hype adjectives, no emojis
- [ ] No borrowed phrasing or scaffolding; swapping the company name out would not make it read as generic
- [ ] Ends on a concrete 10 and 20 year takeaway stated as a specific claim, not a restatement
- [ ] Around 300 to 400 words, comfortably under 3,000 characters (check the count)
- [ ] One single brag-worthy takeaway, named in the file header, that the whole piece ladders to (not written as a line in the post)
- [ ] Has a simple 10 and 20 year outlook that interlinks the brain, one relatable comparison over a pile of numbers, projections labeled as bets
- [ ] Sounds human: contractions, varied rhythm, one genuine first-person beat, no press-release lines
- [ ] Awareness score on the player is still "obscure" or "building" (if "mainstream", pick a fresher hook)
- [ ] The hidden player has not been used as a lead before
