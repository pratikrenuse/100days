# Impulse Space

#player
**Proves:** [[Compute and Resources Become Geopolitics]] (primary)
**Awareness:** Obscure to building (well known inside the space industry and to defence procurement, essentially unknown outside both; the founder's name carries more recognition than the company's)

## One line
Every plan to put data centres in orbit is priced as a launch cost. Getting there is the cheap part. Going anywhere afterwards is a separate company, and it just raised eight hundred million dollars.

## The chokepoint it owns
**Movement between orbits.** A rocket does one thing: it throws mass into a particular place and lets go. That place is almost never where the payload needs to end up, and the payload cannot get itself there without its own propulsion.

This is the step that every orbital claim in this brain assumes and none of them costs. [[Starcloud]], [[Orbital]] (100,000 satellites filed), SpaceX's Starmind, Blue Origin's 51,600-satellite filing: all of them are priced on dollars per kilogram to low Earth orbit. But a constellation of heavy satellites has to reach a **specific** orbit and plane, hold station against atmospheric drag for years, be repositioned as the network changes, and be deorbited at end of life. Every one of those is propellant, and propellant that has to be carried up and then spent.

Impulse builds the vehicles that do it: **Mira**, a precision manoeuvring orbital transfer vehicle already operating in orbit; **Helios**, a high-energy kick stage that takes payloads from low Earth orbit to higher energy destinations, with first flight scheduled 2027; and **Caravan**, a rideshare programme.

## Key facts (dated)
- **The round.** **$308M Series D extension announced September 16, 2026**, bringing the **combined Series D to $808M**. Investors in the extension: **137 Ventures, BANNER VC, DFJ Growth, Linse Capital, Lux Capital, Valor Equity Partners.** Proceeds support the orbital vehicle family including Mira and Helios. (GlobeNewswire / Payload / SatNews, September 16, 2026)
- **Valuation, stated carefully.** The **June 2026 Series D was $500M at a $4.26B valuation**, co-led by 137 Ventures and Banner VC with Founders Fund and Linse Capital participating. Several outlets report the September extension at **$5.4B**. That figure is **reported, not confirmed in the company release**, and any use of it must say so. **The safe, well-sourced number is the $808M combined round.**
- **Who built it.** Founded by **Tom Mueller, SpaceX's first employee**, the propulsion engineer who led development of the **Merlin and Raptor** engines that fly on every Falcon 9 and Starship. This is the relevant credential, not a biography detail: the hard part of in-space mobility is engines that restart reliably after months in vacuum, and that is precisely his discipline.
- **Government position, in or adjacent to window.** A **$28M contract extension from the US Space Force** for the **VICTUS SALO 2 and 3** responsive-space missions. And selection into the **National Security Space Launch (NSSL) Phase 3 Lane 1 provider pool** with Helios, making Impulse the **first non-traditional upper-stage developer to serve as a prime contractor** under that programme. That second item is the structurally interesting one: the US government has formally recognised the upper stage as a separable procurement from the rocket underneath it.
- **Where it is.** Redondo Beach, California.

## Why it matters for the future
The 10-year read. Last run this brain filed [[Stoke Space]] because the orbital leg of this thesis rested entirely on one company's cost curve, and a second fully reusable vehicle relieved that. This is the same discovery one layer over, and a more fundamental one: the launch-cost variable everyone argues about is not the whole bill. If a hundred thousand two-tonne satellites need station-keeping, repositioning and disposal for a decade each, in-space propellant is a **recurring** cost for the life of the fleet, against launch which is one-off. Nobody publishing orbital data-centre economics has put that line in the model. The first filer who publishes a cost per delivered kilowatt-year in orbit rather than a cost per kilogram to orbit is the one who has done the arithmetic honestly.

The 20-year read, stated as a bet. Orbits are becoming addresses, and addresses need roads. The value in a mature space economy sits where it sits in a mature terrestrial one: not in the long-haul carrier, which commoditises fast under competition, but in the last-mile network that reaches the actual destination. Launch has two credible reusable suppliers and will get more. In-space mobility has almost none, and the fact that the US Air Force just made an upper stage a prime contract says the government has noticed the split before the market has.

## Awareness watch
Obscure to building. Triggers to move it: the Helios first flight in 2027; any orbital-compute filer ([[Starcloud]], [[Orbital]], Starmind) naming an in-space mobility provider in a filing; or a second large NSSL award. Note that space is a sector where a single successful flight converts obscurity into coverage overnight, so if this is to be used as a hook it should be used before Helios flies.

## The honest risk
Three. First, **Helios has not flown**, and a kick stage that has not flown is a slide deck with a factory attached. Second, defence procurement is lumpy and political, and a large share of the near-term revenue story is government, which makes it a policy bet as much as a technology one. Third, the valuation figure circulating for this extension is not company-confirmed, and this note should not become the place where a reported number hardens into a fact.
