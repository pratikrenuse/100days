# Stoke Space

**Awareness:** Building, lower edge (covered by space trade press and now by general business press because of the size of the round, but still unknown to the AI audience this brain writes for, and the thing it threatens is not understood at all)

## One line
Every plan to put computers in orbit quietly assumes one company keeps making launch cheaper, and this week a second company got a billion dollars to break that assumption.

## The chokepoint it owns
Not yet anything. What it holds is **the option on the single variable every orbital plan depends on**: cost to orbit.

This brain has been tracking orbital compute for months. [[Starcloud]] trained the first AI model in orbit. [[Orbital]] filed with the FCC for 100,000 data-centre satellites totalling 10 GW. SpaceX filed for up to a million satellites and then named Nvidia silicon for Starmind AI1. Blue Origin filed for 51,600. Google's own analysis puts the pencil-out threshold for orbital compute around $200 per kilogram to orbit.

Every one of those plans is a bet on launch economics, and until now launch economics has effectively meant one company's roadmap. That is a single point of failure sitting underneath an entire leg of this thesis, and it has been unexamined.

## Key facts (dated)
- **The round.** **$1B Series E at a $10B valuation**, announced **September 8, 2026**, described by the company as an initial closing. Co-led by **Point72 Ventures** and **Spark Capital**, with General Innovation, Glade Brook Capital, US Innovation Technology, Washington Harbour Partners, Woven Capital and **Y Combinator** participating. Total capital raised to roughly **$2.3B to $2.4B**. Based in Kent, Washington. (Stoke Space / Space.com / GeekWire / SpaceWatch.Global, September 8, 2026)
- **Vehicle one: Nova Pathfinder.** **Three metric tons** to low Earth orbit. First orbital launch targeted **early 2027**. This is a target, not a scheduled flight, and Stoke has **not yet reached orbit**.
- **Vehicle two: Nova Block 2.** **15 metric tons** to LEO in a **5-metre payload fairing**. First launch targeted **2029**. The funding explicitly supports upsizing to this vehicle.
- **The design claim.** **Fully reusable, both stages.** Not first-stage recovery with an expended upper stage, which is the current industry standard. Full reuse is the thing that would move the cost curve rather than trim it.
- **The gap it is aiming at.** Stoke is positioned to fill the role Falcon 9 vacates as it heads toward retirement. That is the commercial logic: a large, proven, mid-lift workhorse slot opening up with no obvious second supplier.
- **Sector context.** 2026 is already a record year for space-tech startup funding, and this round is among its largest. (Crunchbase, September 11, 2026)

## Why it matters for the future
The 10-year read: if Nova Pathfinder reaches orbit in 2027 and Block 2 follows in 2029, then by roughly 2032 the cost-to-orbit curve has at least two independent suppliers pushing it down, and the orbital-compute filings stop being spectrum land-grabs and start being procurable. That converts [[Starcloud]], [[Orbital]] and Starmind from paper claims into scheduling problems. If Stoke misses, the entire orbital leg of this thesis remains a bet on one company's engineering, which is a much weaker claim than it has been written as.

The 20-year read: the reason compute goes to orbit is the same reason it is currently spreading across counties on fibre ([[Celero Communications]]) and buying its own gas plants ([[SB Energy]], [[Lancium]]). Power and cooling on the ground are constrained by grid queues, water, and now consent. In sun-synchronous orbit the solar resource is up to eight times more productive and the heat sink is free. The binding constraints there are launch cost and radiation, and launch cost is the one with a visible curve. A second reusable vehicle is the first evidence that the curve is an industry property rather than one firm's roadmap, and that is what makes orbital compute a policy question rather than a science-fiction one.

## Awareness watch
Building, lower edge. Triggers to move it fully: a successful Nova Pathfinder orbital flight (early 2027 target), any announced launch agreement with an orbital data-centre operator, or a published price per kilogram. A failed first flight moves it the other way and is the more likely single outcome for any maiden launch.

## The honest risk
Stoke has not flown to orbit. Maiden flights of novel vehicles fail routinely, and full second-stage reuse is harder than anything currently flying operationally. "Early 2027" is a target from a company that has raised money against it. The $10B valuation prices a vehicle that does not yet exist in flight. Any post using this player must say plainly that the rocket has not flown.
