A company just raised 875 million dollars on a plan to not use the most rationed part in AI.

..

Every AI chip hits the same limit, and it is not how fast it can think. It is how fast it can read.

A model is a giant pile of numbers. The chip has to pull those numbers out of memory to do anything at all, so memory sets the speed, not the maths. The industry's answer has been a premium type called high bandwidth memory, stacked right beside the processor.

It works. It is also the most fought-over component in the business, effectively sold out into 2027, and it eats roughly three times the factory capacity of ordinary memory for every gigabyte made. It is the reason a Korean memory maker most people cannot name became one of the most valuable companies on Earth.

..

Positron, in Reno, Nevada, decided to build around the cheap stuff instead. Its next chip runs on the same class of memory that goes into phones and laptops. Ordinary, mass produced, available from plenty of suppliers.

The trade is obvious. Less speed per chip, far more memory per dollar, and you can actually buy it. The design holds up to 2 terabytes on a single accelerator.

The round closed on 10 September at a 5 billion valuation. The chip itself does not exist yet, which is worth saying plainly. It goes into production in late 2027, and every performance figure so far is the company's own.

..

Five weeks earlier, a London company raised around 312 million to escape the same memory by going the opposite direction, into tiny ultra fast memory built into the chip and lasers carrying data between the parts.

One went cheaper. One went faster. Both are betting the shortage is a design choice rather than a law of physics.

A shortage only happens because everybody agreed on the same design at the same moment. Today the AI machine is defined by its most expensive component. Tomorrow it may be defined by the one it was built to do without.
