The first American humanoid robot stock will trade under the ticker AGLT. Its CEO celebrated by promising less, not more.

No robot butlers. No android in your kitchen next year. Peggy Johnson, who runs Agility Robotics, spent her IPO press tour saying one thing: our robot Digit does warehouse work, gets paid for it, and that is the whole pitch.

In this sector, that restraint is almost shocking.

..

The deal: Agility merges with Churchill Capital Corp XI at a valuation near 2.5 billion dollars, raising over 620 million, the largest pile of capital ever assembled in humanoid robotics. Foxconn, the company that builds iPhones, put in 200 million of it.

And there is a real business underneath. Over 300 million dollars in signed multi-year orders for Digit. A factory in Salem, Oregon built to turn out 10,000 robots a year.

..

The same thing is happening on the other side of the Pacific, at double speed.

On July 3, China approved Unitree, the world's humanoid volume leader, for its first big robot IPO. Market chatter now puts its value near 14 billion dollars. And CNBC reports the rest of China's robot startups have reached the same conclusion: listing is a must.

Two continents. A few weeks. Physical labour is getting stock tickers.

..

Here is why that matters more than any demo video. A public company cannot hide behind edited clips. It has to publish what a robot costs, what it earns, and how often it breaks, every single quarter. The sector is about to swap its highlight reel for an accounting statement.

Goldman Sachs already pencils the market at 38 billion dollars by 2035, with shipments past 250,000 robots a year by 2030. Bets, not certainties. The filings will tell us fast.

..

Today, a humanoid robot is a viral video with a company attached. Tomorrow, it is a cost line two rivals report against each other, quarter after quarter. Machines that work will end up valued exactly like people who do: on what they actually get done.
