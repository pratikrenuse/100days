A company with six employees has asked the US government for permission to build 100,000 data centres in space.

The startup is called Orbital. It is five months old. It has raised 5 million dollars, which in AI infrastructure terms is a rounding error of a rounding error. Its FCC filing asks for a constellation delivering 10 gigawatts of computing, roughly ten large nuclear plants' worth.

My first reaction was to laugh. Then I looked at who else has filed.

..

SpaceX asked the FCC in January for up to one million compute satellites, a hundred gigawatts. Starcloud, backed by Nvidia, already has an H100 chip running in orbit, trained the first AI model in space last December, and launches its next satellite, a hundred times more powerful, this October.

The sky is filling up with paperwork.

..

The logic underneath is sober. This month a US heatwave forced data centres onto backup generators under a federal emergency order, so the grid on the ground is already rationing. In the right orbit the sun never sets, so a solar panel earns up to eight times more than it does on Earth. And the vacuum is a free heat sink: no water, no cooling towers, no neighbours.

The catch is the ride. None of this works until launch costs fall to roughly 200 dollars per kilogram, the threshold Google uses for orbital data centres to pencil out. That is Starship-scale economics, and Starship is still proving itself.

..

The filings taught me what is actually scarce here. Not chips, not even power: orbital slots and radio spectrum. Claims are being staked on paper years before the hardware exists, the way railroad barons once drew tracks across land they had never seen.

..

So this is the opening of a land registry, not a product launch. Today, a data centre is an address with a water bill and a grid connection. Tomorrow, some of them are licence numbers overhead, owned by whoever filed first.
