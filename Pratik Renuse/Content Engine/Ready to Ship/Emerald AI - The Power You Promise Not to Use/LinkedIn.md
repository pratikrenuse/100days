The fastest way to get electricity for a data centre right now is to promise you will hand some of it back.

..

Getting connected to the grid in the US has turned into a queue measured in years. Four to seven in Northern Virginia, Phoenix and Dallas. Texas stopped taking new data centre connections outright in August, pending an audit.

The reason is unglamorous arithmetic. A utility plans your connection around your worst moment, not your average one. Tell them you will draw 500 megawatts flat, forever, no exceptions, and they have to find 500 megawatts of brand new firm capacity before they can say yes.

But a lot of AI work is not actually urgent. Training runs can ease off for twenty minutes. Overnight jobs can slide. Only some of it genuinely cannot wait.

If you can prove you will step back on the worst afternoon of the year, you are a much smaller ask.

..

Emerald AI is twenty two months old and sells exactly that: software that decides, in seconds, which jobs can be slowed and by how much.

On 16 September it co-founded an industry alliance with Google and Nvidia, with Anthropic, National Grid, Constellation and NRG among nineteen launch partners. Companies form trade bodies when there is a negotiation to win, and the thing being negotiated here is not price. It is queue position.

The proof so far is one hot afternoon in Phoenix, with Oracle, Nvidia and the two local utilities watching. When the grid strained, the site cut its draw by a quarter and held it there for three hours.

One site, three hours. That is a demonstration, not a habit, and the leap from it to a national fleet is enormous.

..

Every other answer to this problem involves building more power: turbines, reactors, fusion, deeper wells. All of them pour concrete.

This one pours none. It just uses less at the exact minute the grid cannot spare it.

A megawatt is a megawatt, and megawatts keep getting built. What runs short next is the promise attached to them, and the customer who can promise the most gets plugged in first.
