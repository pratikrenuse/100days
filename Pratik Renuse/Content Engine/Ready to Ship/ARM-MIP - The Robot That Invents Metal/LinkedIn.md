America is going to spend 25 million dollars on a laboratory where the metallurgist is a robot. And here is the unusual bit: any researcher in the country will be able to book time on it.

..

Robot laboratories are not new. Machines that mix, heat, break and measure without a human in the room have existed for years.

What has been true until now is that you had to work somewhere rich enough to own one. Which meant a few large companies and a handful of universities. Everyone else queued behind a graduate student and a furnace.

Turning one into a shared national facility is a different kind of decision. It is the difference between owning a printing press and there being a library.

- 24.9 million dollars from the National Science Foundation, over six years, at Texas A&M.
- Described as the first national self-driving laboratory for metals, open to researchers nationwide.

To be clear, this is money and a plan. The building is not running yet, and the first real proof will be an alloy nobody thought of.

..

Metals are an odd thing to get excited about until you notice what sits on them.

The blades inside the gas turbines AI data centres are waiting years for are an alloy problem. So are reactor vessels, and the cores of the transformers that connect them to anything. So are the magnets in most electric motors and most humanoid robots, which lean on a supply chain China almost entirely controls.

A magnet that needs less of the scarce ingredient is not a chemistry paper. It is a foreign policy event.

..

And metallurgy has always moved at the speed of a human career. Try a mixture, wait, measure, publish, retire. A lifetime buys you a hundred or so real attempts.

We have been calling the resulting limits physics. Some of them are just a search that no one has ever had the throughput to finish, run by people who could only afford so many guesses.

Today, the ability to search for new materials belongs to whoever can afford the machine. Tomorrow it belongs to whoever can get a booking.
