At twenty past midnight on July 4th, in the Idaho desert, a new nuclear reactor came alive for the first time.

Construction started under eight months ago. Ground broken in the autumn, chain reaction by Independence Day. Nobody has built a reactor that fast in America in about 80 years.

..

The company is Aalo Atomics, a startup most people have never heard of. And the timing matters more than the fireworks.

That same week, a heatwave pushed America's biggest grid past a demand record that had stood since 2006. The government issued emergency orders, and for the first time ever, data centres were told to run on their backup generators so homes could keep the lights on.

That is the AI boom right now. The machines are ready. The electricity is not. Gas turbines are sold out into 2030, and Virginia just started taxing every kilowatt hour a data centre drinks.

..

Aalo's answer is to stop treating reactors as construction projects and start treating them as products. Small 10 megawatt units, built factory-style, bundled into 50 megawatt pods that sit beside a data centre. Its second reactor is already going up next door to the first, and that one will power a real data centre on site.

..

The part that got me is the deadline. Washington set a goal: at least three new-style reactors running by July 4, 2026. Four made it. In an industry that has missed every deadline for fifty years, that may be the most radical number of the week.

These are test reactors, not power plants. The gap between a first chain reaction and commercial gigawatts is still years wide. Call it what it is: a bet.

..

The pipeline says it is not a one-off, though. About 22 gigawatts of small reactors are in development across the US, and Amazon alone wants 5 gigawatts of them by 2039.

Today, a data centre begs the grid for power and gets switched off in a heatwave. Tomorrow, it arrives with its own reactor in the parts list. Power stops being something you wait for and becomes something you order.
