A rocket throws you incredibly hard in roughly the right direction, and then it lets go.

..

Where it lets go is almost never where you needed to be.

Every headline about putting data centres in space is priced the same way, in dollars per kilogram to get up there. That is the fare to the airport. It is not the ride home.

A satellite has to reach one exact orbit out of thousands of nearby ones. Then it has to stay there, which is not free, because even that high up there is enough air to drag it down, so it spends fuel for years just standing still. Then it gets moved when the network changes. Then somebody has to bring it down safely at the end.

None of that is the rocket's job, and none of it appears in the numbers anyone publishes.

..

Impulse Space took another 308 million dollars on 16 September, bringing that single round to 808 million. It was founded by Tom Mueller, who was employee number one at SpaceX and built the engines that fly on every Falcon 9.

Its small craft, Mira, is already up there moving things around. The bigger one, Helios, flies for the first time in 2027.

The US Space Force put Helios into its national security launch pool, which makes Impulse the first company to hold a prime contract for an upper stage without building the rocket underneath it. The government has started buying the two halves separately. Its June round valued the company at 4.26 billion dollars, and that was before this one.

Helios has not flown yet. Everything above is a plan with a factory behind it.

..

A fleet of a hundred thousand heavy satellites, each spending propellant to hold its slot for a decade, is not a launch bill. It is a fuel bill that arrives every year, forever, and it is missing from every orbital data centre pitch I have read.

Launch is a one off. Staying put is a subscription.

Getting off Earth is on its way to being a commodity with several suppliers. Staying exactly where you meant to be has almost none. In shipping, in telecoms, in parcel delivery, the long haul went cheap and the last mile kept the money.
