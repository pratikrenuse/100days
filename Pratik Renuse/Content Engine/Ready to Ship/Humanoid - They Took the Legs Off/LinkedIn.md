A London robot company raised 152 million dollars last Tuesday at a valuation of 1.35 billion. Its robot has no legs. That is the reason it got the money.

The company is called Humanoid, which makes it nearly impossible to search for, and that is the least interesting thing about it.

..

The round was led by Prime Movers Lab. But the two names that matter on the list are Schaeffler and Bosch. Car parts companies. And neither of them is only an investor.

- Schaeffler is the customer. A binding deal signed in May puts a four figure fleet of these robots into its own plants by 2032, starting with box handling in Germany this December.
- Schaeffler is also the parts supplier. It provides more than half the robot's joints through 2031, a seven figure number of them.
- Bosch is the factory. It has reserved capacity to build up to 100,000 of these machines over five years.

The investor is the buyer is the parts maker. That is not a funding round, it is a supply chain agreeing with itself.

..

Which brings us to the missing legs.

Walking on two legs burns a large share of a robot's computing power on nothing but staying upright. Put the same machine on wheels and all of that goes into the hands instead. It also makes European safety certification far simpler, which Humanoid is aiming to clear in 2027.

Joints are roughly 40 to 60 percent of what a humanoid costs to build. Schaeffler sells joints. It naturally prefers the version that ships sooner and needs fewer of them.

..

Six days later Tesla told investors that Optimus production is still at zero, with roughly 10,000 unique parts to work through first.

IDC expects wheeled robots to grow around 120 percent a year against 95 percent for the walking kind. On those curves the first million robots holding down real jobs will not walk anywhere.

Today everyone is waiting for a machine that moves like a person. Tomorrow the machines doing the work will roll past on wheels, and the word humanoid will turn out to have been a marketing decision.
