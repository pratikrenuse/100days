Every plan to put data centres in orbit rests on one assumption, and almost nobody says it out loud. That a single company keeps making launch cheaper.

..

The orbital pitch is less silly than it sounds. In the right orbit a solar panel is up to eight times more productive than on the ground, because there is no night and no weather. And you never pay to cool anything, because space does it for free.

The paperwork is already filed. SpaceX has applied for up to a million satellites. Blue Origin for 51,600. A six person startup filed for 100,000 of them carrying 10 gigawatts of computing. Nvidia is designing the computer that flies on the first one.

..

All of it turns on a single number: what it costs to lift a kilogram off this planet. Google's own analysis puts the point where orbital computing starts to make financial sense at around 200 dollars a kilo.

And that number has effectively been one company's roadmap. Which is a strange foundation for an industry.

..

On 8 September, Stoke Space raised 1 billion dollars at a 10 billion valuation, co-led by Point72 Ventures and Spark Capital. The company sits in Kent, Washington, and it is building a rocket where both stages come back, not just the first one. That is the harder version, and it is the one that moves the cost curve rather than trimming it.

Its first vehicle carries 3 tonnes to orbit and is aimed at early 2027. The larger one, 15 tonnes, at 2029.

Said plainly, because it should be: Stoke has not reached orbit. First flights of new rockets fail all the time, and this is a target rather than a scheduled launch.

..

Still, the assumption sitting under every space computing story has stopped being about one company. That is not a small change.

Today the cost of leaving Earth is one firm's plan. Tomorrow it is a market price, and the moment it is a price, somebody quotes it for a data centre.
