The most profitable quarter in chipmaking history arrived with a confession.

On July 16, TSMC, the company that manufactures the chips inside almost every AI system on earth, reported 40 billion dollars of revenue in three months, up 36 percent in a year, with record profit. Then it told everyone where the real problem sits.

Not the chips. The gluing.

..

An AI chip is not actually one chip. It is a processor plus stacks of memory, placed closer together than the width of a human hair, bonded onto a slab of silicon so they can talk fast enough. That bonding step is called packaging, it happens in its own factories with its own machines, and TSMC just named it the constraint on the entire AI buildout.

Nvidia queues for it. AMD queues for it. Every tech giant designing its own chip queues for the same lines.

..

So the most famous chipmaker on earth is spending like the future depends on its least famous step. Packaging capacity is growing about 80 percent a year, from 35,000 wafers a month at the end of 2024 toward 130,000 by this December. The day after earnings, TSMC lifted its Arizona plans to 265 billion dollars, with four factories aimed at this one step.

And it still will not close the gap. Even at that pace, supply ends the year about 10 percent short of demand.

..

What struck me is the pattern underneath. First the squeeze was making chips. Then the memory beside them. Now the stitching between them. Each time a layer gets fixed, the pressure moves one floor down, to something more boring, and whoever owns that floor collects.

The next floor is already visible: connections made of light instead of copper, forecast to grow from a rounding error today to about a third of the market by 2030.

Today, the AI race is limited by how well we stitch chips together. Tomorrow, by the light we shine between them. The famous layer keeps changing. The pattern never does.
