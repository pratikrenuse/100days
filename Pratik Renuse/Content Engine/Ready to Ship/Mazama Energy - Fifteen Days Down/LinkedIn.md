A crew in Oregon drilled nearly two miles into a volcano this month, and did it about 80 percent faster than they managed the same hole last year.

..

Every answer to AI's power problem has a waiting list attached.

Gas turbines are sold out past 2029. New reactors need a licence, a fuel supply and years of paperwork. Fusion is a 2030s proposition at best. Solar needs a battery beside it to run through the night.

Heat is the odd one out. It sits under almost everywhere, it runs at three in the morning, and it does not care about weather. You still need a permit, but it is a drilling permit rather than a nuclear licence, which is a difference measured in years. The only other thing separating you from it is how far down you are willing to dig.

Past roughly 374 degrees Celsius, water stops behaving like water or steam and carries far more energy for its weight. Reach that, and one hole does the work of several.

..

Mazama Energy raised 135 million dollars on 17 September to keep digging. Last year it hit 331 degrees Celsius at Newberry in Oregon, the hottest anyone has ever run an engineered geothermal well. This month's well went down 10,350 feet in fifteen days and is still going, aiming for 400.

ConocoPhillips and Shell Ventures both wrote cheques, alongside Khosla and Bill Gates's fund.

Oil companies are not buying a climate story here. They are buying the one skill they already have, which is making a hole cheaper every time they make one.

Because that is the real number. Geothermal never had a manufacturing curve like solar panels or batteries, so it stayed expensive. Drilling time is its version of one, and it just fell by four fifths in a single generation of wells.

Nothing from this reaches a data centre before 2029, at 50 megawatts. It fixes none of what is happening right now.

For a century we put power plants where the fuel was, then ran wires to everyone else.

That map is drawn by pipelines and transmission queues. Twenty years of cheaper holes redraws it by geology instead, and the negotiation that decides where the world's computing lives moves from a utility to a drilling contractor.
