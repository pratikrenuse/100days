The tightest shortage in AI right now is not a chip. It is a leftover from zinc mining.

..

Inside a large AI system, thousands of processors have to talk to each other constantly. For years they did that through copper wire. The traffic got too heavy for copper, so the industry started switching to light: tiny lasers firing data between the parts.

Those lasers cannot be made from ordinary silicon. They are grown on a crystal called indium phosphide.

And indium is not something anyone digs for. It comes out of zinc smelting, as a by-product. The world ends up with roughly as much indium as it happens to want zinc.

..

So the supply cannot respond to us. AI can commit a trillion dollars and the smelters will still produce exactly what the zinc market justifies.

The chief executive of Lumentum, which builds these lasers, said last week that the gap between what customers want and what exists has now passed the memory shortage everyone has been writing about for a year. His number is demand running more than 30 percent above supply, even with output climbing fast.

..

The company growing the crystal wafers is AXT, in Fremont, California. Almost nobody has heard of it.

- Its indium phosphide revenue went from 3.6 million dollars in a quarter a year ago to a record 30.7 million.
- China put indium phosphide on its export control list in early 2025, so every shipment needs a permit. Sales now track permits rather than demand.

Nokia looked at the same problem and bought a chip factory in Arizona to convert into an indium phosphide plant. That deal does not fully close until 2029.

..

Every AI shortage so far has ended the same way. Enough money showed up, someone built more capacity, and the queue moved. Slow and expensive, but solvable.

This one is a different species. There is no cheque that makes the world want more zinc.

Today the race is about who makes the fastest chip. Ten years out, it is about who holds a permit for a metal most people cannot pronounce.
