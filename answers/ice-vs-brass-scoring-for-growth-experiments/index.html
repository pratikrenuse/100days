Questions founders ask me

ICE or BRASS: how should I score and prioritise growth experiment ideas?

By Pratik Renuse · Updated 23 November 2026

Short answer

Both turn a long list of ideas into a ranked backlog. ICE scores Impact, Confidence and Ease. BRASS scores Blink (gut reaction), Relevance, Availability, Scalability and a total Score. Use ICE when the team is small and needs speed. Use BRASS when you want to check whether an idea fits your stage and can grow. Either beats picking the loudest idea in the room.

Where scoring fits

In the growth programme I ran with Newchip accelerator startups in 2020 and 2021, experiments were the ninth call. We used the GROWS loop: gather ideas, rank them, outline the experiment, work, study the outcome. Scoring is the R, rank.

ICE

  • Impact: how much could this move the metric we care about?
  • Confidence: how sure are we that it will work, based on evidence?
  • Ease: how easy is it to run, in time and money?

Score each from 1 to 10 and average or multiply. ICE is fast and good for small teams that need to start running experiments this week.

BRASS

  • Blink: the gut reaction of the team, scored quickly
  • Relevance: does it fit our current goal and stage?
  • Availability: do we have the people, tools and data to run it now?
  • Scalability: if it works, can it grow?
  • Score: the total

BRASS is useful when you have many ideas that are easy but not relevant, or relevant but not available yet.

How I use them

  • Score as a team, independently first, then compare
  • Write the evidence behind each Confidence or Relevance score
  • Re-score the backlog every cycle, because what you learn changes the scores

What to log

For each experiment, log the name, a description, the funnel stage it affects, the lead, start and end dates, the success metric, confidence, hours needed from marketing and engineering, potential impact, required setup, priority, result and learning. A spreadsheet or Asana is enough.

Expect failure

At the start, expect about one in ten experiments to succeed. As the team learns, that rises to about one in three. Scoring does not prevent failures. It makes sure the failures are cheap and the wins are big.
